01 We both left our hometown with our entire life in a suitcase

In our early twenties, we both left our home countries behind (Belgium and Brazil) and moved to Barcelona with nothing but a suitcase, a little savings, and a whole lot of uncertainty. No safety net. No family nearby. Just the belief that there had to be more to life than doing things the “normal” way.

02 In 2017, Farah rented a room from Daniel

Daniel was living in a tiny room while he was renting out the bigger rooms of his shared rental apartment. It was actually a walk-in closet with no exterior windows, turned into a bedroom. Farah rented one of those bigger rooms, and that’s how we met: as housemates in a shared apartment, both trying to make ends meet in one of Europe’s most expensive cities.

03 Farah got her own rental apartment (and ended up broke)

After a few months, Farah moved into her own place. After paying a huge deposit and buying the basics, she had only €67.50 left in her bank account. She even found some of her furniture on the street and made a bed from pallets. Rent was high, savings were gone, and things were getting tight. Going back to cold Belgium because the money ran out? She wasn’t going to let that happen. So she got creative.

Farah sublet her bedroom and sometimes slept on the couch, behind a giant beach blanket she hung from a hook above the fridge to the curtain railing by the window. It wasn’t glamorous, but it worked.

04 The “wait a second…” moment: let’s go all in

At first, it was just a way to help pay the rent. But soon we realized… WAIT. We can actually make serious money doing this?!

So we went ALL IN.

We couldn’t afford a team yet, so we both did everything ourselves: cleaning, check-ins, guest messages and maintenance, even watching YouTube tutorials on how to fix a toilet.

05 We built systems and started working smarter, not harder

We set up self-check-in and automated messages, and hired our first cleaner. Every euro we made, we reinvested, and we made sacrifices. We also upgraded the furniture and made every room more beautiful for a better guest experience. Little by little, the business started running without needing us 24/7.

And THAT changed everything.

06 We officially opened a company and even got our dream rooftop apartment

One apartment turned into two, then five.

When the rules became stricter in Barcelona, we adapted. No tourist stays: we offer mid-term stays of 31+ nights, which is 100% legal without needing a license. Literally anyone is allowed to rent out monthly stays in Barcelona (even on Airbnb), but not many people know this.

We don’t own any property. We work with local owners. We make a profit by renting big apartments with many bedrooms that are hard for them to rent out, and subletting the rooms individually with the owner’s permission.

We know how hard it can be to move alone to a new city, because we’ve been there ourselves. In Barcelona, scams are a real issue, and we’ve been through them: fake listings, lost deposits, endless paperwork, and dirty shared apartments with zero organization.

That’s why our mission is to offer a ready-to-move-in home to students, digital nomads and anyone living abroad, so they can simply arrive and start enjoying the city.

07 Then came the big one: our dream villa in Bali

We put all our savings into a very basic villa in Bali. Scary AF. We renovated it, made it beautiful and turned it into a mini luxury boutique hotel with three bedrooms.

We also officially opened a company in Bali and built a team here too. And the wildest part? We now have rental properties in different parts of the world, and we still don’t own a single one of them.

08 We didn’t just build an income stream. We built freedom.

Fast forward almost 10 years: we run a profitable business with multiple properties around the world and a consistent passive income. And we started literally from zero.

We believe that if you put love into your business, it comes right back to you. So we overpay our staff, work with the local communities, and set up our properties to give our guests an amazing 5-star experience. It’s a win-win.

09 Now we teach you how

When we started, we had no idea what we were doing. We learned everything the hard way, by trial and error and a lot of late nights.

This course is everything we wish someone had told us back then. It’s step by step and built from real-life experience, not just theory. Our course is designed to be your shortcut to business success.

Whether you want some extra income, homes in different places around the world, or a business that gives you more freedom, we’ll show you how, so you can build your own property rental business without making the same mistakes we did.

Will it always be easy? No. Is it possible? 100%.

Choose your business model

Even without owning property.

3 sections · about 15 minLesson01
Section 1

Rental arbitrage vs co-hosting vs ownership

If you’ve ever wanted to start your own business but thought you’d need a fortune to begin, this might be exactly what you’ve been looking for. Most people assume a hosting business means buying property, taking out a mortgage or saving for years. It doesn’t.

In reality, some of the most profitable hosts don’t own a single property, and neither do we.

There are different ways to make money as a host.

There are three main business models: rental arbitrage, co-hosting and ownership. With rental arbitrage and co-hosting, you can launch your hosting business without buying property. You don’t need a mortgage or a big down payment. What you do need is strategy and the right systems.

How much you need to start depends on the model. With co-hosting, you can start with almost no money, because you manage someone else’s property.

With rental arbitrage, you do need some budget. Before your first guest arrives, you pay the deposit, the first month’s rent and the furniture to make the place guest-ready.

To give you an idea: for our apartments in Barcelona, we needed roughly €10,000 to get started. Your number depends on the property and the location. It’s still a fraction of what buying would cost, and once you’re booked, you earn it back fast.

Already own a property? Even better. You’re sitting on an asset that can earn you money. By renting it out on a platform like Airbnb or Booking.com, you can earn much more from it, whether it’s a second home, an investment property or even just a spare room.

With the right setup, it can become a steady source of passive income.

Choose the type of stay that fits your goals, your location and your lifestyle.

Mid-term rentals

Guests stay 1 to 6 months (sometimes longer): mostly students, digital nomads, expats, people relocating for work, and travelers on a long vacation.

Short-term rentals

Bookings range from 1 night up to a few weeks: mostly tourists on holiday, plus weekend trips and business travelers.

Shared vs private properties

The second choice is what guests actually book: the entire place, one room, or both.

Private properties

Guests have exclusive use of an entire unit or suite, with a private entrance, bedroom and bathroom.

Shared spaces

Guests rent a room in a larger unit and share the common areas (kitchen, pool, living room).

Good to know: “coliving”. Most people who run shared spaces call it coliving. It’s the same setup: private rooms, shared common areas. In cities with stricter rules and more tension around short-term rentals, presenting your place as a coliving space can make conversations easier.

List both ways

You list the same property in two ways at once: as an entire place, and room by room. This is the format we usually run ourselves.

One property has several listings: one for the whole property and one for each bedroom. A family or a group of friends can book the entire place. A solo traveler or a couple can book just one room and share the kitchen, living room or pool with other guests. Whichever booking comes in first takes the dates.*

*The calendars must be linked. If one room is booked, the whole property is blocked for those dates, and the other way around. Otherwise you risk a double booking.

The boring stuff (that keeps your business safe).

2 sections · about 15 minLesson02
Section 1

Understanding local rules

Rules around property rentals vary widely depending on where you are, but this also offers opportunities. In general, cities with more tourism tend to have stricter regulations due to housing shortages, tourism limits, and local government control.

Cities like Barcelona, Paris, New York City, and Amsterdam often require licenses, limit the number of rental nights, or restrict short-term rentals to certain zones.

That doesn't mean strictly regulated places are a no-go. We currently run multiple successful apartments in Barcelona, one of the most difficult cities for Airbnb. They don’t have a tourist license, but they’re all still legal.

In Barcelona, anyone is allowed to create a listing on a platform like Airbnb without a license, as long as you host stays of 31 nights or more. Not many people know this, and that’s exactly the opportunity.

While monthly stays tend to generate less income per night than short-term bookings, they’re still highly profitable. Plus, they come with fewer guest turnovers, less day-to-day management, and more stability. You might not even need a manager or a full-time team.

On the other hand, some areas are much more flexible and easier to start in. Places like Bali, parts of Mexico, Portugal, and Southern Spain are known for being more open to short-term rentals, especially in tourist-friendly zones or when offering longer stays. This is why choosing the right location is key.

Before you list any property, it's crucial to understand the local laws and regulations in your area. Every city has its own rules when it comes to short-term rentals. Some require specific licenses or permits, others have restrictions on the number of nights you can rent, and some zones may not allow short-term rentals at all.

Section 2

Legal setup: private, self-employed or company?

Let’s be real, no one gets excited about legal paperwork. Luckily, you don’t need a complicated legal setup to get started. But it’s important to know your options and to declare your income from day one.

You have three main options: rent out as a private individual, start as self-employed, or register a company. Which one fits depends on your country, what you offer your guests and how big you want to grow.

Option 1: rent out as a private individual

Many people don’t know this: in many countries you can rent out a place as a private person, without registering as self-employed or opening a company. You simply declare the rental income on your personal tax return once a year, and you can usually deduct costs like rent, utilities, furniture and cleaning.

This usually works when:

  • You only rent out the space. Cleaning between guests, keys and basic maintenance are normal for any landlord.
  • You don’t offer hotel-like services during the stay, such as a reception, daily cleaning, meals, laundry or airport transfers.
  • You keep it small: one or a few listings, and no staff on your payroll.

Once you start offering hotel-like services or grow into a real operation with staff, tax authorities usually see it as a business. Then you need to register as self-employed or open a company, and in some countries you also charge VAT.

Example: how it works
SpainIf you rent out an apartment you own without hotel-like services, you declare it as rental income in your yearly income tax return (IRPF). No need to become autónomo. If you sublet a place you rent (rental arbitrage), the income is also taxed in your yearly return, unless it’s run as a business. Offer hotel-like services and it becomes a business: you register as autónomo and usually charge 10% VAT.
BelgiumIf you rent out one or a few places without extra services, you can do it as a private individual. You declare it on your yearly tax return: for the property, tax is based on its cadastral income (kadastraal inkomen), and if it’s furnished, 40% of the rent counts as income from the furniture. Offer services like breakfast or cleaning during the stay, and do it regularly? Then it becomes a professional activity: you register as self-employed (this can be a side activity, zelfstandige in bijberoep) and usually charge 6% VAT.
United StatesMost hosts report their rental income as rental income on Schedule E, without self-employment tax. If you provide substantial services, like daily cleaning, meals or concierge, it counts as a business (Schedule C).

Always declare your income. Since 2023, platforms like Airbnb and Booking.com share hosts’ income with the tax authorities for every listing in the EU. Renting out as a private individual doesn’t mean tax-free: it just means less admin.

How to double-check before you decide

The rules are different in every country, so don’t rely on one source. Follow these steps to be sure:

  1. Start with AI. Use the AI prompt in Section 1 to get a quick overview. It’s a great starting point, but AI can be wrong or out of date.
  2. Check your tax office’s website. Search for “renting out property” or “rental income” on the official site, for example the Agencia Tributaria in Spain, the FOD Financiën / SPF Finances in Belgium or the IRS in the US. Many tax offices also have a free helpline or chat.
  3. Check your city or region for licenses and registration. That’s a separate question from taxes.
  4. Talk to a local accountant or tax advisor (in Spain: a gestor or asesor fiscal). Tell them exactly what you plan to do: own or rented property, how long guests stay, and whether you offer extra services.
  5. Get the answer in writing. Ask for it by email and keep it with your documents.

Section 1

Overcoming fear of getting started

Getting started is often the hardest part. It’s completely normal to feel uncertain, nervous, or even overwhelmed when stepping into something new. You might be thinking, “What if something goes wrong?” or “Am I really ready for this?” These doubts are common, but they’re also what keep most people stuck on the sidelines.

The truth is, no one feels fully ready in the beginning. Every successful host once stood exactly where you are now: unsure, questioning themselves, and wondering if they could actually do it. What made the difference is that they took action anyway.

Many people also feel resistance to pitching landlords, afraid they’ll say no. But many owners want exactly what you offer: rent paid on time and a well-kept property. You only need one yes, and Lesson 6 gives you the scripts.

If you want to succeed, you need to shift your mindset from being just a host to thinking like a business owner. Becoming a 6-figure host isn’t just about giving someone a nice place to sleep. If you want to make real money, it’s about creating systems, solving problems, managing relationships, and building something that can grow. A business owner makes decisions based on vision and long-term goals, not just today’s to-do list.

You don’t need to have it all figured out. You just need to start. Follow the steps in this course, one task at a time. You’ll learn by doing, gain confidence as you go, and build momentum with every small win.

Yes, you’ll face challenges. And yes, mistakes will happen. But every problem has a solution, and every obstacle is an opportunity to get better. Confidence doesn’t come from waiting until you’re ready. It comes from showing up anyway.

So take a deep breath, trust yourself, and begin. You’re not just launching a listing. You’re building a business that can change your life.

Section 2

Creative ways to make extra money

Being a host isn’t just about charging for a night’s stay. You can boost your income by offering extra services and experiences, many of which run on their own. Here are some creative ideas:

  • Local partners and referral commissions: Work with local businesses and earn a commission every time you send them a guest. Think of tour operators, surf lessons, motorbike rentals, restaurants, and websites with referral (affiliate) programs for event tickets and activities. Your guests get trusted recommendations and you get paid for them. We earned commission, for example, selling tickets for Barcelona parties to our guests. It was around €10 per ticket, and in summer about 60% of our guests bought them, because it was an activity made for them.
  • Airport transfers: Guests often prefer a smooth arrival, especially in unfamiliar destinations. Partner with a reliable local driver to offer airport pick-up. Add a commission for yourself or bundle it as a premium add-on.
  • Floating breakfasts (especially in Bali): If your property has a pool, a beautiful floating breakfast is a popular experience. It creates shareable content for guests and earns you extra without much work. You can partner with a local café or set up your own simple breakfast tray.

The key is to think about what fits naturally with your property and your lifestyle. These add-ons can increase your income, improve guest satisfaction, and build a more memorable hosting brand with very little extra effort.

Do market research

Find out where guests want to stay, what they pay and when they come.

4 sections · about 15 minLesson04
Section 1

Location vs demand

what actually drives bookings

Most people think location is everything. And yes, being in a prime spot helps, but it's not the full story. A great location with low demand will struggle. A less obvious location with high demand and the right setup? That can become a goldmine.

Location matters, but demand is what drives bookings.

  • Location is fixed. It’s where your property is.
  • Demand is dynamic. It’s what people are searching for, when, and why.

Demand comes from two places. Sometimes people want to be in a certain area. And sometimes they want a certain property, wherever it is.

1. Demand for the area

This is the classic case. Guests come for the city, the beach, a university, a hospital or an event, and they look for a place to sleep nearby. Here you compete with every other listing around you, so what counts is who comes, why, and when.

  • A beach town 20 minutes from a popular city might outperform a central apartment if it taps into weekend getaways and local tourism trends.
  • A quiet suburb near a hospital, university, or big event venue can have consistent bookings if you target the right audience (traveling nurses, researchers, digital nomads, etc.)

2. Demand for the property itself

This is the part most new hosts miss. Many guests don’t travel for the city at all. They travel for the stay. They are not looking for a bed close to the supermarket or the metro. They are looking for an experience, and they are happy to drive an extra hour to get it.

When the property is the reason for the trip, a location that looks “bad” on paper stops being a problem. It can even become the selling point: quiet, private, surrounded by nature, far from everything.

  • A cabin or treehouse in the woods, an hour from the nearest city, that couples book for a weekend with no phone signal.
  • A villa in the middle of rice fields, 20 minutes from the beach and the restaurants, that guests choose for the view, the private pool and the silence.
  • A design house in a small village with nothing around it, booked for birthdays and anniversaries because of how it looks in the photos.
  • A farm stay or countryside house with a hot tub, a fireplace and animals, where families come to switch off for a few days.

In all of these, nobody searched for the village. They found a place they wanted to stay in, and then planned the trip around it. This also means less competition: you are not one of 300 similar apartments in the same neighborhood, you are the only place like yours.

So which one do you have?

Don’t just chase the obvious tourist hotspots. For every property you look at, ask:

  • Who is coming here, and why?
  • Are they coming for the area, or would they come for this property?
  • When is demand highest?
  • What kind of stays are most common: short trips, weekend getaways, monthly relocations?
  • If the location is average, what could make the stay special: a view, a pool, a hot tub, the design, total privacy?

Use AirROI and Airbnb’s own search filters to explore demand in specific areas. Look at occupancy rates, average nightly rates, and competition. Also search Airbnb for unusual stays in your region and check how booked their calendars are.

The goal isn’t just to be in a “good area.” The goal is to have the right property for the right guest: either in a place people already want to be, or special enough that people come for it.

Section 2

How to use AirROI

to analyze profit potential

Before committing to a property, you need to know one thing: Will it actually make money?

AirROI is the tool we recommend for this research. It shows you what a short-term rental can earn in a specific place, based on real booking data. Its calculator and market data are free to use.

Step-by-step:

  1. Go to AirROI.com and open the Calculator
  2. Type in the address (or the area) you're researching and the number of bedrooms
  3. AirROI compares it with around 50 comparable properties nearby and shows you:
    • What a short-term rental there could earn
    • Month-by-month income projections
    • The comparable listings it used
  4. Add your operating costs to go from income to net income
  5. Open the market map to explore the area and compare neighborhoods
  6. Take the nightly price and occupancy you found into your Profit Calculator

Even with great data, don’t rely on just one source. Cross-check with Airbnb itself, Booking.com, or local Facebook rental groups. If two sources give you very different numbers for the same property, be careful and use the lower one.

Understanding the numbers upfront is key to building a profitable hosting business. Use the data, not guesswork, to guide your choices.

Section 4

Research directly on Airbnb

The best free research tool is Airbnb itself. Before you commit to a property, search your area the way a guest would. In twenty minutes you see what you are really competing with, what guests pay and what they care about.

Step-by-step:

  1. Open Airbnb and search your area as a guest. Zoom the map in until it only shows the neighborhood you are looking at.
  2. Set the filters to match your property: entire place or room, number of bedrooms, number of guests.
  3. Check prices for different dates: a weekday and a weekend, one month ahead and three months ahead, high season and low season. Write down the low, typical and high price each time.
  4. Open 5 to 10 listings that look like yours and check their calendars. Many blocked dates in the coming weeks usually mean good demand. Keep in mind that a blocked date can also be the host blocking it, so look at several listings, not just one.
  5. Look at the number of reviews and when the last ones were written. A listing with many recent reviews is getting booked regularly.
  6. Study the best listings in the area, especially the ones marked Guest favorite: their cover photo, title, amenities and price. That is the standard you have to match or beat.
  7. Read the reviews, good and bad. Guests tell you exactly what they loved and what was missing. Both are ideas for your own listing.
What to look for
  • A gap in the market: lots of similar apartments, but nothing for groups, nothing with a workspace, nothing with a pool? That gap can be your listing.
  • Too much competition: hundreds of similar listings with open calendars and low prices is a warning sign.
  • The price guests really pay: the total a guest sees can include cleaning and service fees. Compare totals, not only the nightly price.

Do this search again every few months, also after you launch. Prices, competition and what guests expect change all the time.

Section 1

How to find the right property

Good news: you don’t need special “Airbnb-friendly” listings to get started. For rental arbitrage, you can simply look at normal rental listings on normal property websites and pitch your idea to the owner. You rent the place the normal way, but you come with a better offer. How to pitch it, you’ll learn in the next lesson.

Keep in mind that for many homeowners, this will be the first time they hear about something like this. That’s completely normal. Your job is to explain it clearly and make them feel safe.

1. Start with the legal rules

Before you start looking, make sure you know the rules in your area (see Lesson 2). Check what’s allowed for short-term and mid-term stays, and whether you need a license. Some cities only allow short stays with a license, while mid-term stays of 31+ nights are fine (like in Barcelona). Subletting always needs the owner’s written permission in the contract.

2. Look on the right platforms

You can find suitable properties on:

  • Normal property rental websites, like Idealista, Fotocasa or your local equivalent
  • Facebook Marketplace & local groups (look for flexible landlords)
  • Furnished and mid-term rental platforms like Spotahome or Flatio
  • Real estate agencies (ask for owners who want a reliable long-term tenant)
  • Airbnb itself (reach out to owners who don’t live there; some are open to co-hosting deals)
  • Word of mouth or local connections (especially helpful in places like Bali or Portugal)

Section 2

Red flags to avoid when choosing a property

Not all properties are good for renting out to guests, whether you host short-term or mid-term stays, even if they look beautiful, cheap, or perfectly located. Some places come with hidden issues that can cost you time, money, and a ton of stress. The key is learning to spot these red flags before you commit.

Here’s what to watch out for:

1

Strict building rules or HOAs

If you’re looking at an apartment in a building with a homeowner’s association (HOA), make sure to ask about their rules for short-term rentals and subletting. Some buildings ban or heavily restrict short stays, while mid-term stays of a month or more are often fine. With short stays, a concierge or nosy neighbors will notice guest traffic quickly.

Red flag: Contracts that mention “no subletting,” “no commercial use,” or restrictions on guest frequency.
2

Uncooperative landlords or property managers

If a landlord seems uncomfortable with your plan or won’t give you permission in writing, don’t move forward. Vague verbal approvals won’t protect you if things go wrong. You want a clear agreement up front.

Red flag: Landlords who avoid the topic, say “let’s just see how it goes,” or refuse to put anything in writing.
3

Problematic neighbors or thin walls

Angry neighbors are one of the fastest ways to lose a listing. If you’re sharing walls with people who hate noise, guests, or strangers in the building, you’re asking for complaints and potential legal issues.

Red flag: Past conflicts in the building, complaints from other tenants, or extra-thin walls.
4

Bad layout or limited privacy

Guests value privacy, functionality, and comfort. Avoid properties with awkward layouts, no doors between sleeping areas, or bathrooms that feel exposed. This can lead to bad reviews and poor occupancy rates. Mid-term guests, like digital nomads, also need a good place to work and fast Wi-Fi.

Red flag: Studios trying to act like 2-bedrooms, shared bathrooms, or no soundproofing.
5

Hard-to-reach locations or no parking

Location isn’t just about being central. It’s about being convenient. If the property is hard to find, far from transport, or has zero parking, it could turn off potential guests. Mid-term guests, like students and remote workers, also want shops, cafés and public transport nearby.

Red flag: Remote areas with no public transport or city apartments with expensive or no parking options.
6

Too much personal stuff or damage

Properties that are still full of the owner's belongings, expensive artwork, or delicate furniture can be a headache. Hosting requires a space that’s guest-ready, durable, and easy to clean.

Red flag: Cluttered units, sentimental or fragile items, and spaces that aren’t fully “handed over.”
7

No track record

If the unit has never been rented before and the landlord is unsure, treat it as a test. Try a shorter lease, or ask for a trial period to minimize your risk.

Red flag: High rent, no data to support demand, or landlords who expect full-year commitment without flexibility.

Bottom line: It’s better to walk away from a risky deal than to regret it later. Trust your gut, do your research, and don’t compromise on legal clarity, location quality, or guest experience. A solid property sets the foundation for a stress-free, profitable hosting business.

Section 3

Calculate costs and profit potential

What it really costs to start (& how to keep it low)

One of the biggest questions people have is: How much does it actually cost to start a hosting business? The good news is, it’s probably a lot less than you think, especially if you’re not buying property.

If you’re using rental arbitrage, your main costs will be the first month’s rent, security deposit, and furnishing the space. If you’re co-hosting, your costs are close to zero since you’re working with someone else’s property. Either way, you can start lean and grow smart.

Here are the typical startup costs for rental arbitrage:

  • First month’s rent
  • Security deposit (usually one month)
  • Furniture and essentials
  • Cleaning supplies
  • Photography (can be DIY with a good phone)

To keep costs low, start simple. You don’t need luxury furniture or fancy interior design to impress guests. You need cleanliness, comfort, and functionality. Use secondhand platforms like Facebook Marketplace, buy in bundles, or furnish slowly and improve as you earn.

Also, focus on what truly matters to guests: a good bed, fast Wi-Fi, a clean space, and clear communication. These are what get you bookings and five-star reviews.

Starting small and reinvesting profits is exactly how we built our business. Don’t overthink it, don’t overspend, and don’t try to make it perfect. Launch smart, keep it simple, and upgrade as you grow.

Pitch landlords

If you want to legally sublet (rental arbitrage).

4 sections · about 25 minLesson06
Section 1

Why landlords say yes

(& how to position your offer)

Most people assume landlords will say no to subletting. But here’s the truth: many say yes when your offer is positioned the right way.

Why? Because you're solving a real problem for them.

Most landlords want exactly three things: reliable income, a well-maintained property, and as little stress as possible. Traditional long-term tenants can bring risks like late payments, poor communication, damage, or even legal disputes.

With rental arbitrage, you offer a smart alternative:

  • Consistent, on-time payments
  • Professional cleaning after every guest
  • Ongoing maintenance and inspections
  • Hands-off, well-organized management

When you're pitching to a landlord, keep this in mind: if they’re renting out a property, they likely own at least two (one to live in and one to rent). That often means they’re business-minded or entrepreneurial.

They may be more open than you think, especially when they see that you treat this like a serious business, not a casual side hustle.

Your success as a host depends on keeping their place in top condition because bad reviews kill bookings. That means it's in your best interest to take better care of their property than most long-term tenants ever would.

To build trust, show your systems. Walk them through the cleaning checklist, guest communication templates, and SOPs (you’ll find them all in this course). Show them you’re not just winging it, you’re running a well-organized, guest-focused business. And that gives them peace of mind.

Most importantly, make it clear that you deeply respect the neighbors and community. You screen guests, respect the house rules, and maintain quiet hours because happy neighbors mean long-term success. Position your offer right, and you’re not just asking for permission. You’re offering a win-win.

Focus on their biggest fear: bad tenants

Most people think landlords would never allow this. Why would they let you rent a place just to sublet it on platforms like Airbnb? But smart landlords would actually love to work with professional renters. Why? Because you solve their biggest headaches.

Now compare that to renting to you, a professional renter. The landlord can count on:

  • Guaranteed rent, no late payments
  • A property kept in top condition
  • Professional cleaning after every stay
  • Instant expert repairs in case of damage
  • Exterior security cameras
  • Guest screening protocols
  • Regular property inspections

Your biggest argument to convince a landlord

The better you look after the property, the more 5‑star reviews you get, the more profit. You need the home to stay in excellent condition for this to work.

It’s a win-win: they get zero headaches, and you get a home you can legally sublet for profit. But it won’t work if you just message them: “Hi, can I do Airbnb?” You have to sell the idea, show them the benefits and make it an easy yes. You might message 10 landlords and get only 1 yes, but 1 yes is all you need.

Who you’re actually pitching to

Think about who rents out a second home. Often it’s a business owner or someone who has done well financially. People like that are often more open-minded about a business idea, and they understand the value of someone who runs things professionally.

Your first thought might be: “Which owner would ever allow this?” But honestly, with a good pitch and a clear explanation, it’s very possible. Owners say yes more often than you think.

Don’t use the word “Airbnb” in your pitch. For many owners it’s a trigger word with a negative image: parties, noise and wear and tear. Airbnb is just one of the platforms you might use. Talk about what you actually do: renting out a furnished place to verified guests, with rent guaranteed and the property professionally looked after.

Before you pitch: two case studies

What you pitch matters as much as how you pitch it. If you email every one- and two-bedroom flat on the market, you’ll hear “no” over and over, because those are exactly the places a landlord can rent to anyone. The fastest yes comes from the property the landlord struggles to rent. Here are two real cases from our own business, in two very different markets, so you know what to look for before you send a single email.

Case study · EuropeBarcelona: big apartments, rented room by room

Why landlords say yes here

In Barcelona, what works best for us are big apartments: five, six, sometimes even seven bedrooms. For the owner, these are hard to rent out:

  • The rent is too high for one person or a couple.
  • Finding a group to share it is hard, and the owner wants calm, reliable people, not a flat full of parties.
  • Families rarely want it. It’s a lot of rooms, they usually don’t live in the busy, touristic centre, and few can afford the rent plus the furniture.
  • They usually come unfurnished, so they can sit empty for months.

That’s your opening. You rent the whole apartment, furnish it, give it a fresh coat of paint, and rent out the rooms separately: as coliving or mid-term stays, or short-term where the law allows it. The owner gets the entire apartment rented on one contract, rent on time, and a home that is painted and maintained far more often than with a normal tenant.

What it costs

As private individuals, we used to pay around €1,500 to €1,700 a month for a big apartment. Today, the same type of apartment rents for €2,000 to €2,500. Renting through a company, you add 21% VAT on top: a five-bedroom apartment at €2,000 a month costs us €2,420 a month including VAT.

Our example: from 4 to 6 bedrooms

One of our latest apartments is very central. When we took it, it had 4 bedrooms, a big living room and kitchen, but only 2 bathrooms for all those rooms. Before the laundry area at the back there was a large, wasted room with only cupboards in it.

  • We turned the laundry area at the back into a shower room: 2 bathrooms plus 1 shower room.
  • We moved the laundry into the wasted room with cupboards.
  • The living room was huge, so we built 2 extra bedrooms in it.

Result: an apartment with 4 bedrooms became an apartment with 6 bedrooms. Always agree changes like these with the owner in writing before you start, and check what needs a permit.

What to look for in Europe

  • At least 3 bedrooms, ideally 4 or 5.
  • The rent per bedroom: divide the monthly rent by the number of rooms you can rent out.
  • Furnished or unfurnished: an empty apartment means a furniture budget, but often a cheaper rent and a more motivated owner.
  • Enough bathrooms for the number of rooms, or space to add one.
  • Wasted space, like an oversized living room, that can become an extra bedroom.
Case study · AsiaBali: an easier yes, but the layout decides

A completely different market

In Bali, it’s much easier to convince the owner of a house, or a floor of a house, to rent it to you so you can rent it out on platforms like Airbnb, as long as you have your company and paperwork in order. The number of bedrooms matters much less: one, two, three or four bedrooms can all work, as long as the rent makes sense for the return you can get.

For room-by-room, choose the layout carefully

For the format we like best, renting rooms separately (coliving or short stays), we still look for at least 3 bedrooms we can rotate. And we look closely at how the property is built. We have another property with 2 bedrooms, for example, and for this format it’s not a great fit: the architecture is much more family-style and private, so it doesn’t work well for strangers sharing it.

Our example: 3 suites around a pool

Our 3-bedroom property is built perfectly for renting rooms separately. Downstairs, 2 bedrooms open onto the pool, each with its own bathroom. Upstairs there is a third bedroom, also with its own bathroom. So: 3 suites, all around the pool, with the kitchen separate at the entrance.

That layout lets us run 4 listings for one property: one for each room and one for the entire villa (the “List both ways” format from Lesson 1). Surprisingly, the entire villa has been booked only once. Almost every booking is for the rooms separately.

What to look for in Bali

  • A rent that fits your numbers, whatever the number of bedrooms.
  • At least 3 bedrooms if you want to rent room by room.
  • A private bathroom for every bedroom.
  • Bedrooms that don’t open into each other, with shared spaces like the pool and kitchen apart from where people sleep.

Know your market before you pitch. In Europe, go for the big apartments landlords struggle to fill. In Bali, almost any property with the right numbers can work, but the layout decides whether room by room works. Pitch the right property and you’ll hear yes much sooner, instead of collecting dozens of no’s and giving up.

Section 2

Handling objections

You’ve found a great property. You’re ready to pitch. But the landlord isn’t convinced… yet.

Welcome to the part where most people freeze or give up. Don’t. Objections are normal and often, they’re just questions in disguise. When you understand what landlords are really worried about, you can answer with confidence and turn hesitation into a yes.

Common objections (and how to respond)

Tap an objection to see how to respond. Say your answer out loud before you open it. These are also your cheat sheet for the conversation.

“Isn’t subletting illegal?”

Not if it’s in the contract. Explain that they give you written permission to sublet in the rental contract, so everything is official. You follow the local rules (for example by offering mid-term stays of 31+ nights where short stays need a license) and you manage everything professionally: respectful guests, cleaning and noise control.

“What if guests damage the apartment?”

Explain that you’ll take out rental insurance and that you work with a professional handyman who fixes anything quickly. You only accept ID-verified guests, and you keep the apartment in excellent condition at all times, simply because you need great reviews to keep it booked. Damage is rare, and if something happens, you handle it. The owner won’t be bothered.

“I don’t want parties or problems.”

Tell them about your strict guest screening, house rules, and no-party policy. You use smart tools like noise monitors and security deposits to prevent issues.

“I don’t want random people in my property.”

Totally understandable. All guests are ID-verified, and they’re not party travelers: they’re often professionals staying for work or longer visits. You have strict house rules and you’re always reachable if anything comes up.

“I prefer a long-term tenant.”

You give them the same stable income, with more care for the property. The rent is guaranteed and on time, and if they ever want to go back to a traditional tenant, you handle the transition.

“Will this upset the neighbors?”

You screen every guest, enforce quiet hours and respectful behavior, and there’s always a local contact for emergencies and a direct line of communication.

“Why don’t you just rent it normally?”

Because this is a business, not a side hustle. Explain how your model gives the landlord stable rent, while you handle all the headaches of guest turnover, cleaning, and communication.

You can also show them what they gain compared with a classic long-term contract, where they are tied to the same tenant for years. With this model they keep flexibility: they don’t have to wait for a long contract to end to get their property back. If they have an emergency or simply need the property, they can ask for it back with a much shorter notice period. And on top of that, you take out rental insurance.

Section 4

Closing the deal

Pro tips for closing the deal

  • Speak their language. Don’t sell the dream of hosting. Sell peace of mind. Focus on how you’ll take care of their property, pay rent on time, and be the best tenant they’ve ever had.
  • Use social proof. Show examples of your listings, reviews or experience. If you’re new, share what you’ve learned and your plan. Confidence builds trust.
  • Show that you know the rules. Explain exactly what’s allowed in their area (for example mid-term stays of 31+ nights) and how you’ll follow it. Owners relax when they see you’ve done your homework.
  • Show your systems. Bring the tools from this course: your property manual, cleaning and inspection checklists, contracts for your team and your house rules. It shows you run this professionally.
  • Offer a win-win. Think about what the landlord wants most: no stress, steady income, a well-kept property. Your offer should check those boxes clearly.
  • Know your stuff. The more confident you are, and the better you know the official rules and how everything works, the more owners will trust you.
  • Always get it in writing. Once they say yes, have a simple agreement ready to sign. It protects both sides and shows you’re serious.

Put it in writing

A yes only counts once it’s on paper. What needs to be in writing depends on your business model:

Rental arbitrage: add a subletting clause to your rental contract. Your contract must clearly say that you’re allowed to sublet the property. Without that clause, subletting isn’t permitted, even if the owner agreed in person. You can use this as a starting point:

“The landlord authorizes the tenant to sublet the property, in whole or in part, to third parties for short-term and mid-term stays, and to advertise it on online rental platforms. The tenant remains fully responsible for paying the rent and for the condition of the property.”

Ask a local lawyer or your rental agency to check the wording, because contract rules are different in every country.

  • Co-hosting: you don’t sign a rental contract. Instead, sign a co-hosting agreement with the owner that covers your tasks, your commission, who pays for what, and how either of you can end it.
  • Buying a property: you don’t need anyone’s permission to rent it out, but check the building or homeowners’ association rules and the local license rules before you complete the purchase.

You’re not begging for permission. You’re offering a professional service. Be respectful, but firm. Be clear, but flexible. You’ll hear plenty of no’s, but with the right pitch and the right attitude, you will get that yes.

Pricing strategies

to stay competitive from day one

Setting the right price is one of the most important factors for your success, especially when you're just getting started. If your price is too high, your listing won’t get traction. Too low, and you might fill your calendar but leave money on the table.

2. Use dynamic pricing tools

Section 1

Setting expectations early

Setting clear expectations upfront is one of the most effective ways to avoid misunderstandings, prevent complaints, and ensure a smooth experience for both you and your guests.

Before a guest even books, your listing should clearly communicate exactly what they’re getting. This includes things like:

  • Whether the space is private or shared
  • Quiet hours and noise potential
  • Rules about visitors, smoking, or pets
  • Layout of the space and shared areas
  • Location details (e.g., if it’s in a lively area or a quiet neighborhood)
  • Any limitations or quirks (e.g., steep stairs, construction nearby, no elevator)

It might feel like you’re pointing out negatives, but transparency builds trust. It also protects you. If issues come up, you can always point back to what was clearly mentioned before booking.

Here’s where you should reinforce expectations:

  • Your listing description and house rules
  • The welcome message right after booking
  • Signage or reminders in the property (when necessary)

It’s okay to repeat the same things several times, especially if guests share common spaces.

Being honest doesn’t scare good guests away. It filters out the ones who aren’t the right fit, and those are often the ones who create problems. Setting expectations early makes your life easier, your reviews better, and your hosting stress-free.

How to make guests follow your rules: make them clear
  1. Write rules that are specific. “Check-out is strictly at 11:00 AM” works. “Please be respectful” does not.
  2. Put them in your house rules. Guests have to accept them before booking, so you can always refer back to them.
  3. Repeat them. In the listing description, in the welcome message, and on signs or labels inside the property.
  4. Keep them short. Guests are tired when traveling and ignore long texts.
  5. Be polite but firm when a rule is broken. Point to the rule they agreed to.

Example: shared spaces

We mention in our listing seven times that the kitchen and pool are shared, plus one final reminder in the welcome message. And still, occasionally, a guest arrives claiming we didn't mention it and asks for a full refund.

The reality is: some people simply don’t read. But that’s their responsibility, not yours. As long as you’ve clearly stated it in multiple places, you’re fully protected. If you can prove it was communicated upfront, guests will accept that they made a mistake, and Airbnb will side with you.

Example: construction noise

If something could affect the guest experience, like construction noise nearby, frame it as a benefit rather than a problem. For example, mention in your listing that the current rate already includes a special discount due to temporary construction in the area.

This way, guests feel like they’re getting a deal. Often, you don’t even need to reduce your price significantly. Just highlighting that a discount has been applied makes guests think, “I’m getting a great offer,” rather than focusing on the inconvenience. It's all about how you position it.

Section 1

Hiring an agency or creating your own team

We personally started by doing everything ourselves, not because we wanted to, but because we didn’t have the budget for a team in the beginning. And honestly, it was one of the best ways to learn the business inside out. But let’s be real: cleaning toilets and fixing light bulbs isn’t what grows your business.

While it’s helpful to understand how every part works, the manager role is by far the most important. That’s the person who keeps operations running, guests happy, and problems solved.

Our recommendation: start small but strategic. Begin by hiring one cleaner (and make sure you also have a backup option), and find a reliable freelance handyman who can handle basic maintenance. You don’t have to hire them officially. If you rent out as a private individual or as self-employed, you can work with them as freelancers. Just put your agreement in writing: that’s always better for both sides.

Then, for the first few weeks, manage the listing yourself. Respond to guests, handle check-ins, deal with minor issues, and file damage requests with Airbnb.

This will teach you the platform, the guest journey, and the kinds of things that actually come up.

Once that’s running smoothly and you know what the job involves, you’ll be in a much better position to hire a manager. You’ll understand how long tasks take, what tools they need, and what quality looks like.

Ideally, your manager should live near the property so they can also serve as the emergency contact, someone who can be on-site quickly if there’s ever an issue while you’re away.

This setup gives you the best of both worlds:

  • You keep profit margins high
  • You stay in control
  • You gradually remove yourself from daily tasks without losing oversight
Remember: you don’t need to work in your business. You want a business that works FOR you!

We personally prefer this approach over using an agency because it allows us to build our own team with people we trust and vibe with, while still making the business fully passive over time.

The best part? You stay in control. You set the systems, the standards, and the guest experience.

With an agency, you’re tied to their team, their tools, and their way of working which may not match your vision or priorities. That’s why if you’re considering going the agency route, it’s crucial to find one that aligns with your values and expectations.

It’s also worth running the numbers: compare what you’d spend paying your own team (cleaners, manager, handyman) vs. how much you’d lose by handing over 15–30% of your income to an agency.

You might find that keeping things in-house or semi-in-house gives you more freedom and more profit without sacrificing quality.

Touristic tax

Dividends income tax

Label system

What to label, how to do it, and a printable label sheet.

Bonus★

Labels are one of the cheapest ways to save time. They keep your team organized and answer guests’ questions before they ask. More about this in Lesson 11.

Clear labeling helps your team stay organized, reduces guest confusion, and saves time during turnovers.

Why labels matter:

  • Ensure all items go back in the right place
  • Help new team members quickly understand the system
  • Make your space more intuitive for guests
  • Avoid loss or misuse of supplies
  • Reduce daily questions and communication

What to Label:

For guests:

  • Fridge shelves (label with room/suite names or numbers)
  • Kitchen cabinets or drawers (clearly mark shared vs. private)
  • Water dispensers (e.g. “Drinking Water Only”)
  • Trash & recycling bins
  • Which program you recommend on the washing machine
  • Bathroom storage areas (“Shared” / “Private”)

For staff use:

  • Cleaning product shelves or boxes
  • Supply restock bins (e.g. “spare towels”, “toilet paper stock”)
  • Lost & Found box
  • Garden tools or pool supply storage
  • Instructions for wash cycles on the washing machine
  • Laundry room baskets (e.g. “dirty towels”, “stained sheets”, “ready to wash”)

Label Tips:

  • Use laminated or waterproof labels in kitchens, bathrooms, and outdoor areas.
  • For visual clarity, include icons or color codes if your team includes non-native speakers. Here is a website with free icons that you can use: www.flaticon.com
  • Make sure all labels are consistent in font, size, and wording.
  • Labels sometimes need to be renewed. Therefore, it's useful to invest in a laminating machine or label maker. It doesn't need to be an expensive one, just a simple one will do.
  • KEEP IT SIMPLE! Stick to clear, simple wording: e.g., “Suite 1 Shelf”, “Refills Only”, “Not for Guests”. Don’t add multiple languages, because too much text gets overwhelming. Use one language (for example English) plus icons or images.

Little details that save big headaches

  • Label the washing machine programs so your team knows exactly which setting to use. A ‘WASH’ label on the right program stops guests from picking a 3-hour cycle.
  • Label how things work, like the coffee machine or the heater, especially in shared apartments.
  • Label shelves, drawers and fridge sections in shared kitchens and bathrooms, so every guest knows their space.

Bonus · for shared properties

Declutter shared spaces

A simple 3-step process, with ready-to-send messages.

Bonus★

In shared spaces, guests leave things behind: food in the fridge, bottles in the shower, random items in the hallway. This 3-step process keeps everything clean and fair. More about shared spaces in Lesson 11.

Tip: it’s good to do this at least every 3 months.

Over time, shared spaces like the kitchen and bathroom can get cluttered with items left behind by previous guests or tenants, especially in longer stays or co-living setups. This creates mess, confusion, and even hygiene issues.

To maintain a clean, welcoming space for everyone, it’s important to periodically reset and declutter. Here’s a simple system you can use to clear shared areas without causing misunderstandings or tension.

There are two main approaches that we recommend to avoid removing items that are still in use.

The “lost & found” system

Designate a specific spot for unclaimed or leftover items, like a basket in the bathroom or a drawer in the freezer. Let guests know that anything placed there will be removed after a set deadline.

This method works especially well for things like shampoo bottles, freezer items, or personal belongings left in shared spaces.

The label system

Place a roll of tape and a marker near shared storage areas (like the fridge door or pantry). Ask guests to label their items with their name. Anything left unlabeled will be cleared out.

Key areas to declutter:

  • Shampoo and soap bottles in the shower
  • Freezer and fridge door
  • Unlabeled drawers and shelves
  • Random items in common areas

1. Start with a deep clean

Start with a full cleaning of shared areas, especially the kitchen, bathroom, and hallway. This is the best time to spot forgotten items, expired food, or things taking up unnecessary space. Depending on the decluttering method you choose, either place these items in a designated removal spot or start labeling them with tape and a marker.

2. Communicate clearly & kindly

Even if you've spoken to someone in person, follow up with a message to set clear expectations and avoid any surprises. Include a short deadline and explain the decluttering method to identify or collect their items. Make sure to send a reminder the day before you are removing the items.

3. Set a clear removal date

Give a 48–72 hour window for people to claim their belongings. After that, remove everything left behind. Optional: allow remaining items to be “adopted” before discarding eg: this is the last chance to collect your items. Anything still here after [time] is free to be taken or will be discarded.
