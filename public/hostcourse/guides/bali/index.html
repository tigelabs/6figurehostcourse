BaliHost Guide

+ Bali investor toolkit

How to legally invest, build and run a profitable villa rental in Bali, even as a foreigner.

by Farah & Daniel

Welcome to Bali

Imagine waking up to the sound of the jungle, walking barefoot to your own pool and knowing that the villa around you pays for itself, and then some. Bali can give you that. But Bali also eats unprepared investors for breakfast.

This guide is the add-on to our 6-Figure Host Course. The course teaches you how to launch, run and scale a hosting business anywhere. This one goes deep on a single island: the rules, the land, the paperwork, the builders, the rain, the water and the trash. Everything we wish someone had told us before we signed our first lease in Bali.

Section 1

What you will learn

In Part 1, we help you understand the island: the numbers, the areas, how land ownership works for foreigners and why the colour of a piece of land changes everything.

In Part 2, we set you up legally: your PT PMA company, your investor visa and the licences you need to rent short-term without risking a closure notice.

In Part 3, we buy, build and run: how to spot a badly built villa, how to build one that lasts, the aesthetics Bali guests book, and the day-to-day reality nobody posts on Instagram.

“In Bali, the right partner saves you more money than any negotiation.”
Section 2

The honest truth before you start

Bali is one of the most rewarding places in the world to run a holiday rental. It is also one of the easiest places to lose money if you treat it like a holiday. Here is what nobody tells you in the villa tours:

!

You cannot own land

As a foreigner, you can lease it, use it, or hold it through a company, and each option has rules.

!

The colour of the land matters most

More than the view. Build on the wrong zone and your villa can be sealed, fined or, in the worst cases, demolished.

!

Many villas are built to be sold

Not to last. Cheap materials and badly filled rice fields show up one or two rainy seasons later.

!

Bali is getting stricter

Unlicensed rentals are being delisted from booking platforms and foreigners working on the wrong visa are deported.

✓

The island runs on relationships

Your banjar (village council), your staff and your neighbours matter as much as your paperwork.

Does that mean you should not invest? No. It means the bar is higher, and that is exactly your opportunity. When the rules get tighter, the hosts who did it right get less competition, better rankings and higher prices. Our goal with this guide is simple: help you be one of them.

“In Bali, the cheapest deal is usually the most expensive one.”

Bali by the numbers

And is it still worth it?

3 sections · about 5 minLesson01
Section 1

The demand: tourists keep coming

Before you look at a single villa, look at the market. Numbers do not lie, and in Bali they tell a clear story: demand is still growing.

6.95Mdirect foreign tourist arrivals in Bali in 2025 (BPS Bali).
+9.7%growth in foreign arrivals vs 2024.
23%of foreign visitors came from Australia, the largest source market.
IDR 150Ktourist levy per foreign visitor, in place since February 2024.

On top of foreign visitors, Bali receives millions of domestic Indonesian tourists every year, especially during Indonesian holidays.

Demand is seasonal. The busiest months are usually the European and Australian summer holidays (July and August) and Christmas to New Year. The rainy season, roughly November to March, is quieter outside the holidays, so your pricing strategy has to move with it.
Section 2

The supply: how many places to stay?

Bali has close to 60,000 classified hotel rooms (Colliers, 2023), and that is before you count villas, guesthouses and homestays. AirROI tracked around 34,500 active Airbnb listings in Bali in the 12 months to September 2026. Here is what the average listing achieved:

Metric (12 months to Sep 2026)Bali average
Active listings~34,500
Occupancy38%
Average daily rate~USD 165
Revenue per listing~USD 17,700 per year

Read that again: 38% occupancy on average. Bali is not an island where anything you list gets booked. There is a lot of supply, and a lot of it is average.

The top areas tell a different story. Average revenue per listing in Seminyak was around USD 31,100, in Pecatu (Uluwatu) USD 28,800 and in Pererenan USD 24,300. South Kuta and North Kuta (which includes Canggu) led on occupancy at 44–45%.

Section 3

Why the average is not your target

Averages include the dusty room with no photos, the villa with a broken pool pump and the listing that has not answered a message since 2023. You are not competing with the average. You are competing with the top 20%. In our experience, the villas that consistently outperform in Bali have five things in common:

1

A clear location strategy

They are where their guest wants to be: surfers near the breaks, couples near sunset spots, digital nomads near cafés and coworkings.

2

A design with a point of view

Guests scroll past hundreds of white villas. The ones that get booked have a recognisable look (lesson 10).

3

Private outdoor space

In Bali a private pool is almost the minimum for a villa. A garden, a sunset deck or a rice-field view is what makes it special.

4

Reliable basics

Strong air conditioning, fast Wi-Fi with a backup, hot water, filtered water and no mosquitoes in the bedroom.

5

Legal and visible

Licensed listings will be the only ones left on the major platforms as enforcement tightens. That alone will move rankings.

So, is Bali still worth it?

Yes. But only if you build something better than average.

Part 1 · Lesson 2

Choose your area

Each one is a different business.

4 sections · about 10 minLesson02
Section 1

Canggu, Pererenan & Seseh

Canggu, Berawa & Batu Bolong

Surf, cafés and laptops

The Bali most people picture today: surf, smoothie bowls, beach clubs, laptops and scooters. It went from rice fields to one of the busiest areas on the island in about a decade, and it is still growing.

Who books: digital nomads, young couples, surf travellers and groups of friends. Many stay for weeks, not nights.

What works: 1–3 bedroom villas with a pool, a strong workspace, walking or short scooter distance to cafés and the beach. Monthly stays are a real business here.

Watch out: intense traffic, some of the highest prices on the island, huge competition, and some streets flood in heavy rain.

Pererenan, Seseh & Cemagi

The calmer neighbours

Pererenan has become one of the most wanted addresses for upscale villas: still close to the action, but quieter and greener. Further west, Seseh and Cemagi offer black-sand beaches, sunsets and lower land prices.

This is where many investors are going next.

Our tip: buy where the new roads, cafés and schools are going, not where they already are.
Section 2

Uluwatu, Seminyak, Ubud & beyond

Uluwatu & the Bukit

Cliffs, turquoise water, world-class surf

Uluwatu, Bingin, Pecatu, Balangan, Ungasan. More luxurious and more spread out than Canggu. Who books: couples, honeymooners, surfers and families looking for views and space. Guests pay for the view and the privacy.

Watch out: dry and hot, water supply can be harder (wells or water trucks), long distances. Avoid land too close to cliffs and beaches: in July 2025, authorities demolished 48 illegal structures at Bingin Beach.

Seminyak

The original upscale area

Restaurants, boutiques, beach clubs. Mature, central and still the highest revenue per listing in Bali in the AirROI data. Land is scarce and expensive, so most opportunities are existing villas to renovate.

Ubud

Jungle, culture, wellness

Rice terraces, yoga, temples and cooking classes. Couples, wellness travellers, families and long-stay creatives. What works: jungle or rice-field views, bathtubs with a view, natural materials and silence. Watch out: more rain, high humidity (mould is real) and limited nightlife.

Sanur & the new frontiers

Steady and emerging

Sanur: calm, flat and family-friendly, easy access to the airport. Less “Instagram”, more steady demand. Tabanan coast (Kedungu, Nyanyi): quieter, wilder, cheaper, great surf. Higher risk, higher potential. East and North Bali (Sidemen, Amed, Lovina): beautiful and authentic, but a smaller, slower market. Better as a lifestyle investment than as your first business.

Part 1 · Lesson 3

Who can own what (freehold vs leasehold)

Hak Milik, Hak Sewa, Hak Pakai and HGB explained.

4 sections · about 10 minLesson03
Section 1

Freehold is for Indonesians only

This is the first rule of Bali real estate, and the one most people get wrong: a foreigner cannot own freehold land in Indonesia. Freehold, called Hak Milik, is reserved for Indonesian citizens. Not for foreigners, and not for foreign-owned companies (PT PMA). If someone offers you “freehold”, ask immediately: in whose name?

Never use a nominee

The answer is often a nominee: an Indonesian person who holds the title on paper for you, with a side agreement saying the property is really yours. Nominee arrangements give you no legal protection. If the nominee passes away, divorces, gets into debt or simply changes their mind, the land is legally theirs.

So what can you do as a foreigner? You have four legal routes:

TitleWhat it isDurationBest for
Hak Sewa (leasehold)You rent the land (and villa) from the owner for a fixed termUsually 25–30 years, extension agreed in the contractMost villa investors
Hak Pakai (right to use)Title for foreigners with a KITAS/KITAP to use a residential property30 + 20 + 30 years (max 80)Your own home, not rentals
HGB (right to build)Right to build and own buildings, held by a company like a PT PMA30 + 20 + 30 years (max 80)Larger and commercial projects
Hak Milik (freehold)Full ownershipForeverIndonesian citizens only
Section 2

Leasehold: how it really works

Leasehold (Hak Sewa) is how most foreigners invest in Bali, us included. You lease the land, and usually the villa on it, from the Indonesian owner for a fixed number of years. A few things that surprise people:

You pay upfront

A 25-year lease is usually paid in full on signing, not monthly.

It goes back to the owner

At the end of the lease, the land and everything built on it go back to the owner, unless you extend. That is why the extension clause is the most important part of your contract.

A passport is enough to sign

You do not need a KITAS to sign a lease. But to rent it out commercially, you will need a company and licences (Part 2).

Always a notarial deed

The lease is signed before a notary as a notarial deed (Akta Sewa Menyewa). Never sign a private agreement only.

Think of leasehold like this: you are buying years of income. A 25-year lease on a villa that earns well can be an excellent investment, but its value goes down every year that passes. So when you compare villas, always compare price per remaining year, not just the price.

Section 3

What to put in your lease

A good lease protects you for decades. A bad one can make you lose everything in one family argument. These are the clauses we never sign without:

1

A fixed extension option

With the price agreed today (or a clear formula), plus a right of first refusal if the owner ever sells.

2

Permission to build

To build, renovate and demolish, and clarity on who owns the buildings during the lease.

3

The right to sublease

And to rent commercially (short-term rentals included), and to transfer the lease to a buyer or to your company.

4

Every signature

Of everyone with a claim to the land. In Bali, land is often family land. Spouses and heirs should sign or give written consent.

5

Land tax and disputes

Who pays land tax (PBB) and who handles disputes with neighbours, access roads and the banjar.

6

Access rights

A plot without legal road access is a plot you cannot build or sell properly.

7

The what-ifs

What happens if the land is rezoned, expropriated or the owner passes away.

Pay into escrow

Pay your deposit (often 10–30%) into the notary’s escrow account, never directly to the seller, and only release the rest after the checks are done and the deed is signed.

Part 1 · Lesson 4

Zoning: the colour that decides everything

What you can build and rent, and when to walk away.

4 sections · about 5 minLesson04
Section 1

What the colours mean

Every piece of land in Bali sits in a zone on the official spatial plan (the RDTR, Rencana Detail Tata Ruang). Each zone has a colour on the map, and that colour decides what you can build and whether you can rent it to tourists.

What can I do on this land?
Pick a colourThe result shows here.
Check your exact plotMeant for houses and residential villas, with some small businesses. Commercial short-term rental needs the right licences, and this is where most disputes happen.
Yes, this zone is made for itMeant for hotels, resorts, guesthouses and villas. This is the zone made for tourist accommodation.
Usually yes, with the right licencesHousing combined with commerce and services.
Not intended for villasMeant for shops, offices and restaurants. Check case by case.
NoRice fields and farming. New construction is heavily restricted.
NoForests, rivers, coastlines and sacred areas.

Colours and names can differ slightly between regencies (Badung, Gianyar, Tabanan…), so always read the legend of the map for your area.

Golden rule

Never trust the colour a seller tells you. Check it yourself on the official map and with your notary.

Section 2

How to check the zone of a plot

1

Get the details

The land certificate number and coordinates from the seller (or the exact location pin).

2

Check the official map

The national spatial planning map (Gistaru, from the Ministry of Agrarian Affairs and Spatial Planning) shows the RDTR zones. For Badung, the BATARA app shows zoning too.

3

Ask for a KKPR check

The spatial-use conformity confirmation used in the licensing system. It tells you if your planned activity fits the zone.

4

Visit the offices

The land office (BPN) and the village office, to confirm there are no disputes or special restrictions.

5

Check the surroundings

Distance to temples (sacred areas have extra restrictions), rivers, the coastline and cliffs.

Two more Bali-wide rules: buildings are limited to 15 metres in height (traditionally “no higher than a coconut tree”), and land close to temples falls under sacred-area restrictions defined with the Hindu community.

After the floods of 2025. On 10 September 2025, Bali suffered its worst floods in years, with more than a dozen deaths. Days later, Governor Wayan Koster announced a regulation to bar commercial construction such as villas, hotels and restaurants on productive agricultural land. Before you buy anything on or near rice fields, ask your notary how this applies in your regency today.

Section 3

How zones change in practice

Here is something you will not read in most guides: zone colours in Bali are not set in stone. Spatial plans are revised periodically. When a regency updates its RDTR, land that was green can become yellow or pink, usually where the area around it has already developed. A green plot next to existing villas, roads and yellow or pink zones is more likely to change colour than a green plot in the middle of the rice terraces.

You will also hear sellers say that “this green land can be changed”, sometimes casually, as if it were a formality. Be very careful here.

✓ What protects you
  • Only an official change: a revised RDTR or a confirmed KKPR, in writing.
✕ What does not
  • Promises, “arrangements” and verbal assurances are not a title. What was tolerated yesterday can be enforced tomorrow.
  • Productive rice fields are now the most protected land on the island, especially after the 2025 floods.
  • Never pay extra for a future conversion that has not happened yet.
Our approach

Price the land for the colour it is today. If it changes later in an official revision, that is upside. If you need it to change for your plan to work, it is not a deal, it is a bet.

“Buy the zone you need. Do not buy the zone you hope for.”

Part 2 · Lesson 5

Your PT PMA: the company that lets you do business

The real minimum investment in 2026.

3 sections · about 5 minLesson05
Section 1

What is a PT PMA?

A PT PMA (Perseroan Terbatas Penanaman Modal Asing) is an Indonesian limited company with foreign shareholders. For a foreigner who wants to run a villa rental business legally in Bali, it is the standard route.

Earn rental income legally

Run a business and earn income in Indonesia as a foreigner.

Hold the licences

The NIB and the right KBLI code that booking platforms now ask for.

Hold land rights

Like HGB, or sign leases in the company’s name.

Sponsor your visa

Your investor KITAS, so you can live in Bali and manage your business.

Your PT PMA has at least two shareholders (people or companies), a director and a commissioner, a registered address in Indonesia, a tax number and a bank account. It reports its investment activity every quarter (the LKPM report) and files taxes every month and every year.

It sounds like a lot. In practice, a good set-up agent and a local accountant do 90% of it for you. Your job is to choose them well and understand what they are doing.

Important: the company’s business code (KBLI) must match what you actually do. A company set up for “real estate” or “consulting” cannot legally rent villas to tourists (lesson 7).

Section 2

The real minimum investment in 2026

For years, the number you saw everywhere online was IDR 10 billion (around USD 600,000), and many people gave up on Bali because of it. The rules changed. Under BKPM Regulation No. 5 of 2025, in force since 2 October 2025, there are two different numbers:

IDR 2.5BPaid-up capital: money actually deposited in the company’s bank account (was IDR 10 billion).
IDR 10B+Total investment plan: what the company commits to invest in its business, per business line.
✓ Good to know
  • For property and accommodation businesses, the value of land and buildings can count towards the investment total. Your leasehold, construction and furniture are part of your investment.
  • The paid-up capital cannot be transferred out for 12 months, but it is not frozen: it can be used for operations, buying assets and funding construction.
  • Companies set up under the old rule can apply to reduce their capital to the new minimum.
What the internet says vs what happens

Online: “You need to invest IDR 10 billion to open a company in Bali.” Many people think they must transfer it on day one.

In practice: the IDR 10 billion is an investment plan, not a single wire transfer. You declare it at set-up and report progress every quarter through the LKPM. Your lease, construction, furniture and operations build towards it over the years. What needs to be in the account is the paid-up capital, and even that is there to be used for the business.

Section 3

Questions for your set-up agent

Every agent structures this slightly differently, and the details depend on your business code, your share structure and your visa plans. Before you sign with anyone, ask these five questions:

1

Capital

How much do I need to deposit, when, and what can I spend it on?

2

Investment plan

How will the investment plan be realised and reported (LKPM)?

3

Business codes

Which KBLI codes will my company have, and do they allow short-term villa rental?

4

Visa

How does this structure work with the investor KITAS I want?

5

Yearly costs

What does it cost every year to keep the company compliant (accounting, tax, LKPM, licences)?

Part 2 · Lesson 6

The investor visa (and how people get deported)

Investor KITAS step by step.

3 sections · about 5 minLesson06
Section 1

Bali is strict about your visa

Let’s be very clear: a tourist visa does not allow you to do business in Bali. Not to work in your villa, not to manage staff, not even to promote your villa on social media.

It happens

In September 2024, an Australian on a Visa on Arrival was deported and banned from re-entering Indonesia for six months for promoting a villa on social media. Officials had been monitoring his posts before they arrested him. In 2026, authorities also stepped up checks on content creators and photographers working on tourist visas.

Overstaying, working without the right permit or running a business on the wrong visa can lead to fines, detention, deportation and entry bans. And if your business is in your name and you get deported, you will be managing it from abroad.

✓ On a tourist visa you can
  • Look at properties
  • Meet notaries
  • Sign a lease
✕ But not
  • Work in your villa or manage staff
  • Promote your villa on social media
  • Operate the business in any way
“If you would not post it with your immigration officer watching, you are probably on the wrong visa.”
Section 2

The investor KITAS step by step

The right status for most villa investors is the investor KITAS (visa index E28A): a temporary stay permit for foreigners who own shares in a PT PMA and sit on its board as a director or commissioner.

What you need to know
WhoShareholders of a PT PMA who are director or commissioner
Minimum sharesIDR 10 billion of shares in your own name (not through a nominee or holding company)
Validity1 or 2 years, renewable
Can you work?A director can sign contracts and manage the company. Operational jobs need a separate work permit.
TimingAbout 4–8 weeks after the investment ministry’s recommendation; longer if company documents need changes

Two different tests. The company can be set up with IDR 2.5 billion paid-up capital, but the investor KITAS asks for IDR 10 billion of shares in your name. Ask your agent to plan your share structure with the visa in mind from day one, not afterwards.

Part 2 · Lesson 7

Licences to rent short-term (the 2026 crackdown)

NIB, KBLI, permits and the 10% tax.

5 sections · about 5 minLesson07
Section 1

What happened in 2025 and 2026

For years, many Bali villas were rented on Airbnb without any licence. That era is ending. Here is the timeline:

Jul 2025 48 illegal structures demolished at Bingin Beach
3 Dec 2025 Bali’s governor proposed stopping Airbnb operations

Citing illegal rentals run by foreigners and lost tax revenue.

Dec 2025 Platforms are not banned, but every listing must be licensed

The Ministry of Tourism made it clear: correctly zoned and paying tax.

Feb 2026 Airbnb started asking hosts for their NIB and KBLI
31 Mar 2026 Deadline announced for listings to be licensed
May 2026 About 1,600 unlicensed businesses identified nationwide

Villa registrations were up 76.4% in a year.

1 Aug 2026 Planned start of delisting for operators who did not regularise
Jun 2027 Target for automatic checks of every listing against the national licensing system (OSS)

Nine booking platforms are working with the Ministry of Tourism on this, including Airbnb, Booking.com, Agoda, Expedia and Traveloka. Listings can be restored once the operator is licensed.

Section 2

The licences you need

Requirements depend on your structure, zone and regency, but a legal short-term villa rental in Bali generally needs:

1

A legal entity

A PT PMA (or another legal entity) that operates the villa.

2

NIB

The business identification number, issued through the OSS online licensing system.

3

The right KBLI

Villas (55193), homestays or pondok wisata (55130), other short-term accommodation (55199). Pondok wisata is aimed at local, Indonesian-run homestays and is typically not available to foreign-owned companies.

4

NKU & Sertifikat Standar

The activity number and standards certificate linked to your business, depending on its risk level.

5

PBG & SLF

The building approval and the certificate that the building is fit for use (commercial use where required).

6

Correct zoning

For tourist accommodation (lesson 4).

7

Local tax

Registration (NPWPD) and the 10% hotel and restaurant tax on your rental revenue, reported and paid every month. Airbnb does not pay it for you.

8

Village / banjar

Registration, which in many areas is expected and makes daily life much easier.

Pro tip: when you buy an existing villa, ask for every one of these documents before you sign. A villa that “has been on Airbnb for years” without them is not a proven business. It is a risk.
Section 3

Why stricter rules are good news

It is easy to read all of this and feel discouraged. We see it the other way around. Remember the 34,500 listings from lesson 1? A part of them are not licensed. As enforcement tightens, a share of your competition will simply disappear. The hosts who stay will have:

✓ Less competition for the same tourists✓ Better visibility and rankings✓ Access to all booking channels, corporate travellers and agencies✓ A business they can actually sell one day✓ Peace of mind: no closure notice, no visit from immigration

If Bali gets even stricter in the coming years, and everything suggests it will, a fully legal villa becomes something rare. And rare things are valuable.

“A legal villa in a strict market is not a cost. It is your moat.”

Part 3 · Lesson 8

Buying a finished villa without buying a problem

Built-to-sell villas, rice-field fill and sinking floors.

4 sections · about 5 minLesson08
Section 1

Built to sell, not to last

Walk around Canggu, Pererenan or Uluwatu and you will see villas going up everywhere. Many of them are built by developers whose business model is simple: build fast, build cheap, sell fast.

There is nothing wrong with a developer making a profit. The problem is the incentive: the builder needs the villa to look perfect on the day of the sale and to survive the warranty period, if there is one. After that, it is your problem. In our experience, a cheaply built Bali villa often looks amazing in year one, starts showing problems in year two, and needs serious money by year three. We have seen:

✕

Cracks

In walls and floors a few months after handover.

✕

Leaking roofs

In the first real rainy season.

✕

Pools that lose water

Every day, because of poor waterproofing.

✕

Rusting steel

Reinforcement pushing concrete apart, especially near the sea.

✕

Swollen doors and windows

That stop closing, made from untreated wood.

✕

Mould behind fresh paint

Because walls were never properly waterproofed.

✕

Weak electrics

Too weak for air conditioning in every room.

These are not just repair costs. They are bad reviews, cancelled bookings and lost months while you fix things. A villa with a leak in the bedroom does not get five stars, whatever the design looks like.

Section 2

The rice field under your villa

A huge part of the land being built on in Bali was, until recently, rice fields. Rice fields are wet, soft and designed to hold water. To build on them, the land is filled and raised. When that fill is done properly (the right material, compacted in layers, with time to settle and proper drainage) it can work. When it is done fast and cheap, you get:

Ground settlement

Parts of the villa sink unevenly. Floors tilt, tiles crack, walls split, doors stop closing.

Drainage problems

The plot sits lower than the neighbours or the road, and water flows into your villa in heavy rain.

Pool and terrace movement

Heavy structures on poorly compacted ground move first.

Humidity and mould

From a high water table.

Even if the plot was converted legally years ago, the soil underneath is still the same. Ask questions.

Section 3

How to inspect a villa before you sign

Never buy a villa in Bali based on photos and a tour with the seller. Here is the process we follow:

1

Documents first

Land certificate, lease, zoning, PBG and SLF, as-built drawings, list of materials, warranties and the contractor’s name.

2

Your own inspector

An independent building inspector or structural engineer, never the seller’s. It costs very little compared to what it can save you.

3

Structure

Cracks (especially diagonal ones), uneven floors, signs of settlement, exposed or rusty steel.

4

Water

Roof, gutters, waterproofing of bathrooms and terraces, damp patches, smell of mould, fresh paint in strange places.

5

The pool

Ask for a water-loss test over several days.

6

Materials

Real hardwood or treated timber? Quality of windows, roof tiles, plumbing and electrical cables.

7

Systems

Electricity capacity (kVA), septic tank, water source and pressure, hot water, Wi-Fi.

8

Neighbours & banjar

Floods, disputes, construction planned next door.

Red flag

A seller who does not want you to bring your own inspector, or who gives you a deadline of a few days to decide.

Red flag

Fresh paint and new plaster in only a few spots of an otherwise “old” villa. Ask what was repaired.

Part 3 · Lesson 9

Build your own villa so it lasts 20 years, not 2

The rules we would give our best friend.

1 sections · about 5 minLesson09
Section 1

Build it right from the ground up

Building gives you full control over quality, design and layout. It also gives you full responsibility. These are the rules we would give our best friend:

1

Permits before the first brick

Your PBG must match the zone and your plans. Building first and “legalising later” is exactly what Bali is cracking down on.

2

Start with the soil

A soil test and a foundation designed by an engineer are not optional, especially on former rice fields.

3

Design the drainage first

Raise the plot above the road, plan where every drop of rain goes, and size gutters for tropical downpours.

4

Materials for the climate, not the photo

Treated or durable hardwood, stainless or galvanised fittings near the sea, quality waterproofing in every wet area, good ventilation to fight humidity.

5

Plan electricity early

Agree the capacity with your utility (PLN) early. Upgrades can take time.

6

Pay by milestones

Never upfront. Link every payment to finished, inspected work and keep a retention (often 5–10%) until after the first rainy season.

7

Your own eyes on site

An architect, project manager or inspector who works for you, not for the contractor, and checks every stage.

8

Height and setbacks

Respect the 15 m height limit and setbacks from roads, rivers and neighbours.

9

Schedule around the rain

Foundations and roofs in the dry season if you can.

Plan for delays

Building in Bali almost always takes longer than promised. Plan your cash flow for delays and do not book guests before the villa is finished and inspected.

Part 3 · Lesson 11

Running a villa in Bali day to day

Water, waste, rainy season, staff and ceremonies.

4 sections · about 10 minLesson11
Section 1

Water: filter everything

Bali tap water is not drinkable. Most villas get their water from the local utility (PDAM), from a deep well, or from water trucks in drier areas like the Bukit. Quality varies by area, by season and even by property. After heavy rain it becomes less predictable, and Bali groundwater is also hard (full of calcium and magnesium).

This affects your guests’ health, your reviews, your pool and your appliances. Our advice: install a proper water filtration system in every villa. A typical set-up, chosen after testing your water:

1

Sediment filter

Removes sand, dirt and rust.

2

Activated carbon

Removes smells and many impurities.

3

Water softener

Protects showers, taps, washing machines and water heaters from limescale.

4

UV purification

Neutralises bacteria and viruses.

5

Reverse osmosis in the kitchen

For drinking water, or 19-litre water gallons with a dispenser.

In your welcome notesWe’ve installed a high-quality water filter, so you can shower with clean, skin-friendly water and brush your teeth safely.
Pro tip: schedule filter changes and a water test with your maintenance team. A filter system that is not maintained is just decoration.
Section 2

Waste: the problem nobody shows you

Bali has a serious waste problem, and as a villa owner it is your problem too. The island’s biggest landfill, Suwung in Denpasar, served Denpasar, Badung, Gianyar and Tabanan. From 31 March 2026 it only accepted inorganic and residual waste, and it was scheduled to close completely on 1 August 2026, with a waste-to-energy plant under construction to replace it. During the transition, uncollected trash piled up on roadsides in parts of the island for weeks.

The rainy season makes collection harder: flooded roads, delayed pick-ups, bins that overflow and attract animals and smells. Nothing ruins a five-star stay faster than a pile of trash at the gate.

A reliable private collectorWith a fixed schedule, and a backup.
Separate organic and inorganicCompost organic waste on site if you have the space.
Closed, animal-proof binsStored out of sight, away from the entrance.
Cut plasticRefillable water, bulk amenities, no single-use bottles.
Explain the system to guestsIn your welcome notes. Many come to Bali for its nature, and a villa that handles waste responsibly is part of the experience.
Section 3

Rainy season, power & maintenance

The rainy season runs roughly from November to March. It is green, dramatic and beautiful, and it tests every weakness in your villa.

Before the rains

Clean gutters and drains, check the roof, reseal terraces and bathrooms, trim trees near the roof and power lines.

During the rains

Watch for leaks, flooding in the garden, pool overflow and algae. Have your maintenance person on call.

Humidity & mould

Run dehumidifiers or AC regularly in closed rooms, air out wardrobes, use breathable materials, treat wood.

Power cuts

Keep rechargeable lights, consider a backup for Wi-Fi and the fridge, and make sure guests know what to do.

Pests

Regular termite and pest control, especially for wooden structures and roofs.

Build a monthly maintenance routine with your team (the course has the inspection and maintenance checklists) and add these Bali-specific items to it.

Pro tip: adjust your pricing for the low season instead of fighting it. Monthly stays for nomads and long-stay guests can keep occupancy up from January to March.
Section 4

Your team, your banjar & Nyepi

In Bali, your local relationships are part of your business plan.

Your team

Most villas have a small local team (housekeeping, pool and garden, maintenance) and a manager or management company. Treat them well, pay on time, follow labour rules and give them clear SOPs. Good staff in Bali are worth gold and stay with good owners.

Ceremonies

Balinese Hinduism is part of daily life. Your staff will need days off for ceremonies, and some will place offerings at the villa. Respect it and plan your schedule around it.

Your banjar

The village council manages local matters, from security to community contributions. Introduce yourself, contribute when asked and keep a good relationship. When you need help, they are the first to know.

Nyepi, the Day of Silence

Once a year, the whole island stops for 24 hours. No lights, no noise, no going out, and the airport closes. Tell guests booked on those dates well in advance and prepare food and a cosy kit. For many, it becomes the highlight of their trip.

Taxes every month: remember the 10% local tax on your rental income. Your manager or accountant should report and pay it monthly.

“Bali rewards the owners who become part of the community, not just part of the skyline.”
