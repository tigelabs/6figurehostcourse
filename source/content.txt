<p>Choose the type of stay that fits your goals, your location and your lifestyle.</p>
<div class="opt">

<div class="opt">
  <div class="opt-head"><h4>Short-term rentals</h4><p>Bookings range from 1 night up to a few weeks: mostly tourists on holiday, plus weekend trips and business travelers.</p></div>
  <figure><img src="img/calendar-short-term.jpg" alt="Booking calendar with four listings, each filled with many short bookings from different guests" loading="lazy"><figcaption>A short-term calendar: many guests, each staying a few nights.</figcaption></figure>

<p>Here is how the two compare.</p>
<div class="cmp-wrap"><table class="cmp">
<thead><tr><th></th><th>Mid-term</th><th>Short-term</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>

::task Choose mid-term or short-term stays
::part Shared vs private properties
<p>The second choice is what guests actually book: the entire place, one room, or both.</p>
<div class="opt">
  <div class="opt-head"><h4>Private properties</h4><p>Guests have exclusive use of an entire unit or suite, with a private entrance, bedroom and bathroom.</p></div>
</div>
<div class="opt">
  <div class="opt-head"><h4>Shared spaces</h4><p>Guests rent a room in a larger unit and share the common areas (kitchen, pool, living room).</p></div>
  <div class="opt-note"><strong>Good to know: “coliving”.</strong> Most people who run shared spaces call it <strong>coliving</strong>. It’s the same setup: private rooms, shared common areas. In cities with stricter rules and more tension around short-term rentals, presenting your place as a coliving space can make conversations easier.</div>
</div>
<div class="opt">
  <div class="opt-head"><h4>List both ways</h4><p>You list the same property in two ways at once: as an entire place, and room by room. This is the format we usually run ourselves.</p></div>
  <p>One property has several listings: one for the whole property and one for each bedroom. A family or a group of friends can book the entire place. A solo traveler or a couple can book just one room and share the kitchen, living room or pool with other guests. Whichever booking comes in first takes the dates.*</p>
  <div class="opt-note">*<strong>The calendars must be linked.</strong> If one room is booked, the whole property is blocked for those dates, and the other way around. Otherwise you risk a double booking.</div>
</div>
<p>Here is how the three compare.</p>
<div class="cmp-wrap"><table class="cmp">
<thead><tr><th></th><th>Private</th><th>Shared</th><th>Both ways</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>

::part Legal setup: private, self-employed or company?
<p>Let’s be real, no one gets excited about legal paperwork. Luckily, you don’t need a complicated legal setup to get started. But it’s important to know your options and to declare your income from day one.</p>
<p>You have three main options: <strong>rent out as a private individual, start as self-employed, or register a company.</strong> Which one fits depends on your country, what you offer your guests and how big you want to grow.</p>
<h4 class="tc">Option 1: rent out as a private individual</h4>
<p>Many people don’t know this: <strong>in many countries you can rent out a place as a private person, without registering as self-employed or opening a company.</strong> You simply declare the rental income on your personal tax return once a year, and you can usually deduct costs like rent, utilities, furniture and cleaning.</p>
<p>This usually works when:</p>
<ul><li><strong>You only rent out the space.</strong> Cleaning between guests, keys and basic maintenance are normal for any landlord.</li><li><strong>You don’t offer hotel-like services during the stay,</strong> such as a reception, daily cleaning, meals, laundry or airport transfers.</li><li><strong>You keep it small:</strong> one or a few listings, and no staff on your payroll.</li></ul>
<p>Once you start offering hotel-like services or grow into a real operation with staff, tax authorities usually see it as a business. Then you need to register as self-employed or open a company, and in some countries you also charge VAT.</p>
<div class="cmp-wrap"><table class="cmp">
<thead><tr><th></th><th>Example: how it works</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><th>Spain</th><td>If you rent out an apartment you own without hotel-like services, you declare it as rental income in your yearly income tax return (IRPF). No need to become autónomo. If you sublet a place you rent (rental arbitrage), the income is also taxed in your yearly return, unless it’s run as a business. Offer hotel-like services and it becomes a business: you register as autónomo and usually charge 10% VAT.</td></tr>
<tr><th>Belgium</th><td>If you rent out one or a few places without extra services, you can do it as a private individual. You declare it on your yearly tax return: for the property, tax is based on its cadastral income (<em>kadastraal inkomen</em>), and if it’s furnished, 40% of the rent counts as income from the furniture. Offer services like breakfast or cleaning during the stay, and do it regularly? Then it becomes a professional activity: you register as self-employed (this can be a side activity, <em>zelfstandige in bijberoep</em>) and usually charge 6% VAT.</td></tr>
<tr><th>United States</th><td>Most hosts report their rental income as rental income on Schedule E, without self-employment tax. If you provide substantial services, like daily cleaning, meals or concierge, it counts as a business (Schedule C).</td></tr>
</tbody></table></div>
<div class="pboxes"><div class="pbox"><p><strong>Always declare your income.</strong> Since 2023, platforms like Airbnb and Booking.com share hosts’ income with the tax authorities for every listing in the EU. Renting out as a private individual doesn’t mean tax-free: it just means less admin.</p></div></div>
<h4 class="tc">Option 2 and 3: self-employed or a company</h4>
<p>When you’re growing, offering hotel-like services or hiring a team, it’s time for an official setup.</p>
<div class="tw"><table>
<tr><th>Category</th><th>Self-employed</th><th>Register an official company</th></tr>
<tr><td>Best for</td><td>Getting started quickly with low risk and minimal costs</td><td>Scaling your business, hiring people, and protecting yourself legally</td></tr>
<tr><td>Pros</td><td>• Easy and cheap to register<br>• Less paperwork and admin<br>• Great for testing the waters</td><td>• Separates personal &amp; business assets<br>• More credibility with landlords and banks<br>• Possible tax advantages<br>• Easier to partner or raise capital</td></tr>
<tr><td>Cons</td><td>• No legal separation<br>• You’re personally liable<br>• Might seem less professional</td><td>• More complex setup<br>• Ongoing admin and costs<br>• Likely need an accountant</td></tr>
<tr><td>Ideal for</td><td>If you’re just starting or managing 1–2 listings with services</td><td>If you’re growing fast, managing multiple properties, or hiring a team</td></tr>
</table></div>

::lesson ch4 | 04 | Do market research | Part 1 · Lesson 4 | Do market | research | Find out where guests want to stay, what they pay and when they come.
::part Location vs. demand
::h Location vs. demand | what actually drives bookings
<p>Most people think location is everything in Airbnb. And yes, being in a prime spot helps, but it's not the full story. <strong>A great location with low demand will struggle. A less obvious location with high demand and the right setup? That can become a goldmine.</strong></p>
<h4>Location matters, but demand is what drives bookings.</h4>
<ul><li><strong>Location is fixed.</strong> It’s where your property is.</li><li><strong>Demand is dynamic.</strong> It’s what people are searching for, when, and why.</li></ul>
<p>Demand comes from two places. Sometimes people want to be in a certain area. And sometimes they want a certain property, wherever it is.</p>
<h4>1. Demand for the area</h4>
<p>This is the classic case. Guests come for the city, the beach, a university, a hospital or an event, and they look for a place to sleep nearby. Here you compete with every other listing around you, so what counts is who comes, why, and when.</p>
<ul><li><em>A beach town 20 minutes from a popular city might outperform a central apartment if it taps into weekend getaways and local tourism trends.</em></li><li><em>A quiet suburb near a hospital, university, or big event venue can have consistent bookings if you target the right audience (traveling nurses, researchers, digital nomads, etc.)</em></li></ul>
<h4>2. Demand for the property itself</h4>
<p>This is the part most new hosts miss. <strong>Many guests don’t travel for the city at all. They travel for the stay.</strong> They are not looking for a bed close to the supermarket or the metro. They are looking for an experience, and they are happy to drive an extra hour to get it.</p>
<p>When the property is the reason for the trip, a location that looks “bad” on paper stops being a problem. It can even become the selling point: quiet, private, surrounded by nature, far from everything.</p>
<ul><li><em>A cabin or treehouse in the woods, an hour from the nearest city, that couples book for a weekend with no phone signal.</em></li><li><em>A villa in the middle of rice fields, 20 minutes from the beach and the restaurants, that guests choose for the view, the private pool and the silence.</em></li><li><em>A design house in a small village with nothing around it, booked for birthdays and anniversaries because of how it looks in the photos.</em></li><li><em>A farm stay or countryside house with a hot tub, a fireplace and animals, where families come to switch off for a few days.</em></li></ul>
<p>In all of these, nobody searched for the village. They found a place they wanted to stay in, and then planned the trip around it. This also means less competition: you are not one of 300 similar apartments in the same neighborhood, you are the only place like yours.</p>
<h4>So which one do you have?</h4>
<p><strong>Don’t just chase the obvious tourist hotspots. </strong>For every property you look at, ask:</p>
<ul><li>Who is coming here, and why?</li><li>Are they coming for the area, or would they come for this property?</li><li>When is demand highest?</li><li>What kind of stays are most common: short trips, weekend getaways, monthly relocations?</li><li>If the location is average, what could make the stay special: a view, a pool, a hot tub, the design, total privacy?</li></ul>
<p>Use AirROI and Airbnb’s own search filters to explore demand in specific areas. Look at occupancy rates, average nightly rates, and competition. Also search Airbnb for unusual stays in your region and check how booked their calendars are.</p>
<p>The goal isn’t just to be in a “good area.” The goal is to have the right property for the right guest: either in a place people already want to be, or special enough that people come for it.</p>

::part Research directly on Airbnb
<p>The best free research tool is Airbnb itself. Before you commit to a property, <strong>search your area the way a guest would</strong>. In twenty minutes you see what you are really competing with, what guests pay and what they care about.</p>
<h4>Step-by-step:</h4>
<ol>
  <li>Open <a href="https://www.airbnb.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Airbnb</a> and search your area as a guest. Zoom the map in until it only shows the neighborhood you are looking at.</li>
  <li>Set the filters to match your property: entire place or room, number of bedrooms, number of guests.</li>
  <li>Check prices for different dates: a weekday and a weekend, one month ahead and three months ahead, high season and low season. Write down the low, typical and high price each time.</li>
  <li>Open 5 to 10 listings that look like yours and check their calendars. Many blocked dates in the coming weeks usually mean good demand. Keep in mind that a blocked date can also be the host blocking it, so look at several listings, not just one.</li>
  <li>Look at the number of reviews and when the last ones were written. A listing with many recent reviews is getting booked regularly.</li>
  <li>Study the best listings in the area, especially the ones marked Guest favorite: their cover photo, title, amenities and price. That is the standard you have to match or beat.</li>
  <li>Read the reviews, good and bad. Guests tell you exactly what they loved and what was missing. Both are ideas for your own listing.</li>
</ol>
<div class="card">
  <div class="label">What to look for</div>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>A gap in the market:</strong> lots of similar apartments, but nothing for groups, nothing with a workspace, nothing with a pool? That gap can be your listing.</li>
    <li><strong>Too much competition:</strong> hundreds of similar listings with open calendars and low prices is a warning sign.</li>
    <li><strong>The price guests really pay:</strong> the total a guest sees can include cleaning and service fees. Compare totals, not only the nightly price.</li>
  </ul>
</div>
::tip Do this search again every few months, also after you launch. Prices, competition and what guests expect change all the time.

::part How to find the right property
<p>Good news: you don’t need special “Airbnb-friendly” listings to get started. <strong>For rental arbitrage, you can simply look at normal rental listings on normal property websites</strong> and pitch your idea to the owner. You rent the place the normal way, but you come with a better offer. How to pitch it, you’ll learn in the next lesson.</p>
<p>Keep in mind that <strong>for many homeowners, this will be the first time they hear about something like this.</strong> That’s completely normal. Your job is to explain it clearly and make them feel safe.</p>
<h4>1. Start with the legal rules</h4>
<p>Before you start looking, make sure you know the rules in your area (see <a href="#ch2">Lesson 2</a>). Check what’s allowed for short-term and mid-term stays, and whether you need a license. Some cities only allow short stays with a license, while mid-term stays of 31+ nights are fine (like in Barcelona). Subletting always needs the owner’s written permission in the contract.</p>
<h4>2. Look on the right platforms</h4>
<p>You can find suitable properties on:</p>
<ul><li>Normal property rental websites, like Idealista, Fotocasa or your local equivalent</li><li>Facebook Marketplace &amp; local groups (look for flexible landlords)</li><li>Furnished and mid-term rental platforms like Spotahome or Flatio</li><li>Real estate agencies (ask for owners who want a reliable long-term tenant)</li><li>Airbnb itself (reach out to owners who don’t live there; some are open to co-hosting deals)</li><li>Word of mouth or local connections (especially helpful in places like Bali or Portugal)</li></ul>

::lesson ch6 | 06 | Pitch landlords | Part 1 · Lesson 6 | Pitch | landlords | If you want to legally sublet (rental arbitrage).

::h Why landlords say yes | (&amp; how to position your offer)
@p42-43
<h4 class="tc">Focus on their biggest fear: bad tenants</h4>
<p>Most people think landlords would never allow this. Why would they let you rent a place just to sublet it on platforms like Airbnb? <strong>But smart landlords would actually love to work with professional renters. Why? Because you solve their biggest headaches.</strong></p>
<div class="hd-grid">
<figure><img src="img/obj-dirty.webp" width="387" height="478" decoding="async" loading="lazy" alt="A home left dirty and neglected: regular tenants don’t always treat it well, and the owner only finds out when they move out."></figure>
<figure><img src="img/obj-rent.webp" width="387" height="478" decoding="async" loading="lazy" alt="Late or missed rent: the risk of chasing a tenant for the rent every month. No landlord wants that."></figure>
<figure><img src="img/obj-damage.webp" width="387" height="478" decoding="async" loading="lazy" alt="Damage and deposit fights: who pays for what? Move-out often ends in an argument over the deposit."></figure>
<figure><img src="img/obj-neighbors.webp" width="387" height="478" decoding="async" loading="lazy" alt="Neighbor complaints: noise, parties, people coming and going. And the landlord is the one who gets the angry call."></figure>
</div>
<p><strong>Now compare that to renting to you, a professional renter.</strong> The landlord can count on:</p>
<ul class="hd-ticks"><li>Guaranteed rent, no late payments</li><li>A property kept in top condition</li><li>Professional cleaning after every stay</li><li>Instant expert repairs in case of damage</li><li>Exterior security cameras</li><li>Guest screening protocols</li><li>Regular property inspections</li></ul>
<div class="hd-box"><p class="hd-label">Your biggest argument to convince a landlord</p><p><strong>The better you look after the property, the more 5&#8209;star reviews you get, the more profit. You need the home to stay in excellent condition for this to work.</strong></p><p>That’s why your interests and the landlord’s are the same: a guest who finds a dirty or damaged home leaves a bad review, and bad reviews cost you money. So you take better care of their property than almost any tenant would. Reviews like this one are what you work for:</p><figure class="hd-rev"><img src="img/rev-iveta.webp" width="944" height="514" decoding="async" loading="lazy" alt="Guest review from Iveta, Australia, five stars: Absolutely amazing! The way the place is so clean and customer oriented and detailed is unbelievable. You can see that hosts put their heart in it and it’s great for people who value quality. Absolutely recommend!"></figure></div>
<div class="pboxes"><div class="pbox"><p><strong>It’s a win-win:</strong> they get zero headaches, and you get a home you can legally sublet for profit. <strong>But it won’t work if you just message them: “Hi, can I do Airbnb?”</strong> You have to sell the idea, show them the benefits and make it an easy yes. You might message 10 landlords and get only 1 yes, but 1 yes is all you need.</p></div></div>
<h4 class="tc">Who you’re actually pitching to</h4>
<p>Think about who rents out a second home. Often it’s a business owner or someone who has done well financially. People like that are often more open-minded about a business idea, and they understand the value of someone who runs things professionally.</p>
<p>Your first thought might be: <em>“Which owner would ever allow this?”</em> But honestly, with a good pitch and a clear explanation, it’s very possible. Owners say yes more often than you think.</p>
<div class="pboxes"><div class="pbox"><p><strong>Don’t use the word “Airbnb” in your pitch.</strong> For many owners it’s a trigger word with a negative image: parties, noise and wear and tear. Airbnb is just one of the platforms you might use. Talk about what you actually do: renting out a furnished place to verified guests, with rent guaranteed and the property professionally looked after.</p><p><strong>Talk about coliving instead.</strong> Coliving is becoming more and more popular, and many landlords are open to it, especially now. It’s the perfect fit for the big apartments that owners find hard to rent. If you come as a professional renter and say <em>“I want to set it up as a coliving space”</em>, you will find owners who say yes. And because you rent out the rooms one by one instead of the whole apartment, that’s where your profit comes from.</p></div></div>
<h4 class="tc">Before you pitch: two case studies</h4>
<p>What you pitch matters as much as how you pitch it. If you email every one- and two-bedroom flat on the market, you’ll hear “no” over and over, because those are exactly the places a landlord can rent to anyone. <strong>The fastest yes comes from the property the landlord struggles to rent.</strong> Here are two real cases from our own business, in two very different markets, so you know what to look for before you send a single email.</p>
<details class="obj tpl case"><summary><span><small>Case study · Europe</small>Barcelona: big apartments, rented room by room</span></summary>
<p><strong>Why landlords say yes here</strong></p>
<p>In Barcelona, what works best for us are <strong>big apartments with 4 to 6 bedrooms</strong>. The historic centre is full of them, and owners find them hard to rent out:</p>
<ul><li><strong>The rent is too high for a couple.</strong> A big apartment comes with a big price, more than one or two people want to pay.</li><li><strong>The people who are interested are often the wrong tenants.</strong> Usually it’s a group of young people, and the owner’s fear is that they sublet the rooms illegally or throw parties.</li><li><strong>Families rarely live there.</strong> They usually don’t want to live in the busy, touristic centre.</li><li><strong>They usually come unfurnished</strong>, so they can sit empty for months.</li></ul>
<p>That’s your opening. You rent the whole apartment, furnish it, give it a fresh coat of paint, and rent out the rooms separately: as coliving or mid-term stays, or short-term where the law allows it. <strong>The owner gets the entire apartment rented on one contract</strong>, rent on time, and a home that is painted and maintained far more often than with a normal tenant.</p>
<p><strong>Our example: from 4 to 6 bedrooms</strong></p>
<p>One of our latest apartments is very central. When we took it, it had 4 bedrooms, a big living room and kitchen, but only 2 bathrooms for all those rooms. Before the laundry area at the back there was a large, wasted room with only cupboards in it.</p>
<ul><li>We turned the laundry area at the back into a <strong>shower room</strong>: 2 bathrooms plus 1 shower room.</li><li>We moved the <strong>laundry</strong> into the wasted room with cupboards.</li><li>The living room was huge, so we built <strong>2 extra bedrooms</strong> in it.</li></ul>
<p>Result: <strong>an apartment with 4 bedrooms became an apartment with 6 bedrooms.</strong> Always agree changes like these with the owner in writing before you start, and check what needs a permit.</p>
<p><strong>What to look for in Europe</strong></p>
<ul><li><strong>4 to 6 bedrooms</strong>, at least 3.</li><li><strong>The rent per bedroom</strong>: divide the monthly rent by the number of rooms you can rent out.</li><li><strong>Furnished or unfurnished</strong>: an empty apartment means a furniture budget, but often a cheaper rent and a more motivated owner.</li><li><strong>Enough bathrooms</strong> for the number of rooms, or space to add one.</li><li><strong>Wasted space</strong>, like an oversized living room, that can become an extra bedroom.</li></ul>
</details>
<details class="obj tpl case"><summary><span><small>Case study · Asia</small>Bali: much easier to get a yes</span></summary>
<p><strong>A completely different market</strong></p>
<p>In Bali, it’s much easier to convince an owner to rent a house, or a floor of a house, to you so you can rent it out on platforms like Airbnb, as long as your company and paperwork are in order. The number of bedrooms matters much less: one, two, three or four bedrooms can all work, <strong>as long as the rent makes sense for the return you can get.</strong></p>
<p><strong>How it works: leasehold</strong></p>
<p>Most properties in Bali work with a different system: <strong>leasehold</strong>. You don’t buy the property forever, you buy the right to use it for a fixed number of years, often 25. You pay up front, and when the lease ends, the property goes back to the original owner, together with everything you built or improved on it.</p>
<p>You can also find leases for fewer years. We first took Casa Mobili for <strong>3 years</strong>, with the right to extend written into the contract. Because you’re not paying the price of buying a property, it was still very affordable.</p>
<p><strong>Why we chose Casa Mobili: built for coliving</strong></p>
<p>We chose Casa Mobili because its setup is perfect for our coliving model: a shared pool with individual bedrooms around it. Instead of trying to rent out the entire villa, we divide it up and rent out the bedrooms one by one.</p>
<p>Downstairs, 2 bedrooms open onto the pool, each with its own bathroom. Upstairs there is a third bedroom, also with its own bathroom. So: 3 suites around the pool, with the kitchen separate at the entrance. That lets us run <strong>4 listings for one property</strong>: one for each room and one for the entire villa (the “List both ways” format from <a href="#ch1">Lesson 1</a>). Surprisingly, <strong>the entire villa has been booked only once.</strong> Almost every booking is for the rooms separately.</p>
<p>Not every property works like this. We have another property with 2 bedrooms whose architecture is much more family-style and private, so it’s not a great fit for strangers sharing it. <strong>For coliving, the layout decides.</strong></p>
<p><strong>What to look for in Bali</strong></p>
<ul><li><strong>A rent that fits your numbers</strong>, whatever the number of bedrooms.</li><li><strong>At least 3 bedrooms</strong> if you want to rent room by room.</li><li><strong>A private bathroom for every bedroom.</strong></li><li><strong>Bedrooms that don’t open into each other</strong>, with shared spaces like the pool and kitchen apart from where people sleep.</li></ul>
</details>
<div class="pboxes"><div class="pbox"><p><strong>Know your market before you pitch.</strong> In Europe, go for the big apartments landlords struggle to fill. In Bali, it’s much easier to get a yes, and with leasehold you can start with a short contract; for coliving, choose a layout with private bedrooms around shared spaces. Pitch the right property and you’ll hear yes much sooner, instead of collecting dozens of no’s and giving up.</p></div></div>
::part Handling objections
@p44 until "Common Objections"
::h Common objections (and how to respond)
<p>Tap an objection to see how to respond. Say your answer out loud before you open it. These are also your cheat sheet for the conversation.</p>
<details class="obj"><summary>“Isn’t subletting illegal?”</summary><p><strong>Not if it’s in the contract.</strong> Explain that they give you written permission to sublet in the rental contract, so everything is official. You follow the local rules (for example by offering mid-term stays of 31+ nights where short stays need a license) and you manage everything professionally: respectful guests, cleaning and noise control.</p></details>
<details class="obj"><summary>“What if guests damage the apartment?”</summary><p>Explain that you’ll take out <strong>rental insurance</strong> and that you work with a <strong>professional handyman</strong> who fixes anything quickly. You only accept <strong>ID-verified guests</strong>, and you keep the apartment in excellent condition at all times, simply because you need great reviews to keep it booked. Damage is rare, and if something happens, you handle it. The owner won’t be bothered.</p></details>
<details class="obj"><summary>“I don’t want parties or problems.”</summary><p>Let them know your strict guest screening, house rules, and no-party policy. You use smart tools like noise monitors and security deposits to prevent issues.</p></details>
<details class="obj"><summary>“I don’t want random people in my property.”</summary><p>Totally understandable. All guests are ID-verified, and they’re not party travelers: they’re often professionals staying for work or longer visits. You have strict house rules and you’re always reachable if anything comes up.</p></details>
<details class="obj"><summary>“I prefer a long-term tenant.”</summary><p>You give them the same stable income, with more care for the property. The rent is guaranteed and on time, and if they ever want to go back to a traditional tenant, you handle the transition.</p></details>
<details class="obj"><summary>“Will this upset the neighbors?”</summary><p>You screen every guest, enforce quiet hours and respectful behavior, and there’s always a local contact for emergencies and a direct line of communication.</p></details>
<details class="obj"><summary>“Why don’t you just rent it normally?”</summary><p>Because this is a business, not a side hustle. Explain how your model gives the landlord stable rent, while you handle all the headaches of guest turnover, cleaning, and communication.</p><p>You can also show them what they gain compared with a classic long-term contract, where they are tied to the same tenant for years. With this model they keep flexibility: they don’t have to wait for a long contract to end to get their property back. If they have an emergency or simply need the property, they can ask for it back with a much shorter notice period. And every stay comes with Airbnb’s host protection and insurance on top.</p></details>

<h4>Pro tips for closing the deal</h4>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Speak their language.</strong> Don’t sell the dream of hosting. Sell peace of mind. Focus on how you’ll take care of their property, pay rent on time, and be the best tenant they’ve ever had.</li>
  <li><strong>Use social proof.</strong> Show examples of your listings, reviews or experience. If you’re new, share what you’ve learned and your plan. Confidence builds trust.</li>
  <li><strong>Show that you know the rules.</strong> Explain exactly what’s allowed in their area (for example mid-term stays of 31+ nights) and how you’ll follow it. Owners relax when they see you’ve done your homework.</li>
  <li><strong>Show your systems.</strong> Bring the tools from this guide: your property manual, cleaning and inspection checklists, contracts for your team and your house rules. It shows you run this professionally.</li>
  <li><strong>Offer a win-win.</strong> Think about what the landlord wants most: no stress, steady income, a well-kept property. Your offer should check those boxes clearly.</li>
  <li><strong>Know your stuff.</strong> The more confident you are, and the better you know the official rules and how everything works, the more owners will trust you.</li>
  <li><strong>Always get it in writing.</strong> Once they say yes, have a simple agreement ready to sign. It protects both sides and shows you’re serious.</li>
</ul>
<h4 class="tc">Put it in writing</h4>
<p>A yes only counts once it’s on paper. What needs to be in writing depends on your business model:</p>
<div class="pboxes"><div class="pbox"><p><strong>Rental arbitrage: add a subletting clause to your rental contract.</strong> Your contract must clearly say that you’re allowed to sublet the property. Without that clause, subletting isn’t permitted, even if the owner agreed in person. You can use this as a starting point:</p>
<p><em>“The landlord authorizes the tenant to sublet the property, in whole or in part, to third parties for short-term and mid-term stays, and to advertise it on online rental platforms. The tenant remains fully responsible for paying the rent and for the condition of the property.”</em></p>
<p>Ask a local lawyer or your rental agency to check the wording, because contract rules are different in every country.</p></div></div>
<ul><li><strong>Co-hosting:</strong> you don’t sign a rental contract. Instead, sign a co-hosting agreement with the owner that covers your tasks, your commission, who pays for what, and how either of you can end it.</li><li><strong>Buying a property:</strong> you don’t need anyone’s permission to rent it out, but check the building or homeowners’ association rules and the local license rules <em>before</em> you complete the purchase.</li></ul>

::part How to handle complaints or damages
<p>No matter how well you prepare, at some point some guests will complain or cause damage. <strong>What separates great hosts from the rest is how they respond.</strong></p>
<div class="flow">
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">1</span><div><h4>Stay calm and respond fast</h4><p>Reply quickly and professionally, even if the complaint feels unfair or minor. Acknowledge the issue, thank the guest for letting you know, and tell them you’re working on a solution.</p>
<div class="bubble"><span class="bub-from">You</span>Thank you for sharing this with us. We’re so sorry for the inconvenience and we will do our best to find a solution.</div></div></div>
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">2</span><div><h4>Evaluate and take action</h4>
<div class="vk-two vk-tight"><div class="mini"><b>Minor complaint</b>Offer a solution quickly: send the cleaner again, give a small refund or replace what’s missing.</div><div class="mini"><b>Bigger issue</b>Gather evidence first: security footage, message logs, cleaner reports, or a visit if you’re nearby.</div></div></div></div>
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">3</span><div><h4>Document everything, especially with photos</h4><p>Keep screenshots, pictures and receipts. If there’s damage, take clear photos and file a damage claim through Airbnb <strong>within 14 days or before the next guest checks in</strong>, whichever comes first.</p>
<div class="vk-tip"><b>[[ic:camera]] Important:</b> ask your cleaner to always take photos if something is damaged or missing (even towels). A quick photo of the room before and after each turnover can be your best proof in a dispute.</div></div></div>
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">4</span><div><h4>Be firm when needed</h4><p>If a guest breaks a house rule or causes damage, be polite but direct. Point to the listing and house rules they agreed to when booking.</p>
<div class="bubble"><span class="bub-from">You</span>As stated in the house rules, parties are not allowed. Due to the disturbance and cleanup required, we’ll need to charge an additional fee to cover the extra cleaning and guest compensation.</div></div></div>
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">5</span><div><h4>Don’t take it personally</h4><p>Not every review will be 5 stars, and not every guest will behave perfectly. Learn from each case, improve your systems and keep going. Over time you’ll face fewer problems.</p><p>You never know what someone is going through. Traveling makes people tired, anxious or irritable. Arguing, even when you’re completely right, rarely ends well.</p></div></div>
</div>
<div class="gold"><span class="gold-k">Our golden rule</span><p><strong>Kill them with kindness.</strong> Acknowledge their experience, show understanding and tell them you’ll do your best to help. This simple shift in tone can calm a tense situation and turn a potential conflict into a positive review.</p></div>
<p>Handling complaints or damages is part of the business. The faster and more professionally you respond, the better your reviews and your peace of mind.</p>
::tip At the end of this chapter, you’ll get access to a ready-to-use 'Quick Replies Library' with message templates to handle common issues. You can copy and paste them directly to save time and respond professionally in any situation.
::part How to be a 5-star host
<p>Being a 5-star host doesn’t mean being available 24/7 or bending over backward for every request. It means creating a seamless, thoughtful experience <strong>before, during and after</strong> the stay. Great service is often what separates a 4-star review from a 5-star one.</p>
<div class="jour">
<div class="jour-c"><span class="jour-k">Before check-in</span><h4>Proactive communication</h4><ul><li>Send a warm, clear pre-arrival message: address, check-in time, how to get there and how to get in (see the bonus templates)</li><li>Ask for their arrival time and any special needs</li><li>Answer questions before they ask: Wi-Fi, local tips, what to expect</li><li>Be friendly but professional. You’re setting the tone.</li></ul></div>
<div class="jour-c"><span class="jour-k">During the stay</span><h4>Available, not intrusive</h4><ul><li>Check in after the first night. A simple “Hope everything’s going well! Let me know if you need anything” goes a long way.</li><li>Make sure your welcome booklet or digital guide answers the FAQs</li><li>If a problem comes up, respond fast and keep the guest informed, even if you can’t fix it right away</li></ul></div>
<div class="jour-c"><span class="jour-k">After check-out</span><h4>Leave a lasting impression</h4><ul><li>Thank them for staying and invite them to leave a review</li><li>If you solved a problem, mention it: “Thanks again for your patience with the plumbing issue, we’re glad we could sort it quickly!”</li></ul></div>
</div>
<div class="cherry"><div class="cherry-stars" aria-hidden="true">★★★★★</div><span class="gold-k">The cherry on top</span><h4>Tell them you’re leaving them 5 stars</h4><p>Most guests simply check out and never leave a review. A short message after check-out changes that. When you tell guests they were great and that you’re leaving them a 5-star review, they’re much more likely to review you back, and to give you 5 stars too.</p>
<div class="bubble"><span class="bub-from">You</span>Thank you so much for staying with us! You were amazing guests, and we’ve just left you a 5-star review. If you enjoyed your stay, we’d be so grateful if you left us one too. Safe travels!</div></div>
<div class="tips4">
<div><b>Be human</b>A warm tone beats robotic professionalism.</div>
<div><b>Be clear</b>Clear instructions and expectations reduce stress.</div>
<div><b>Be honest</b>If something goes wrong, own it and offer a solution.</div>
<div><b>Be kind</b>Especially with complaints, empathy always wins.</div>
</div>
<p><em>Customer service isn’t just about fixing problems. It’s about showing guests you care. When they feel cared for, they leave happier, review better and recommend your place.</em></p>

::part Building your own team
<p>When you’re launching your first listing, you can do things yourself. But if you plan to scale, you’ll want a small, reliable team. <strong>Build it in this order:</strong></p>
<div class="flow">
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">1</span><div><span class="jour-k">Hire first</span><h4>A cleaner (and a backup)</h4><p>Guests expect spotless spaces and tight turnovers. A good cleaner keeps your ratings high and saves you from doing all the dirty work. <strong>Always have a backup cleaner.</strong> Life happens, and you don’t want to be stuck when your main cleaner can’t make it.</p>
<div class="vk-tip"><b>[[ic:bulb]]Bonus tip: pay per checkout, not per hour.</b> Every guest is different: some leave the place spotless, others leave a mess. In our shared apartments, we use a hybrid model: a fixed rate for the common areas, plus a fee per room cleaned.<br><span class="tipsub">Smart detail: a washing cycle takes about 1 hour and 15 minutes, so it naturally keeps the cleaner on-site long enough to finish the full turnover properly.</span></div></div></div>
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">2</span><div><span class="jour-k">Next</span><h4>A handyman</h4><p>For small repairs, maintenance and unexpected fixes. You don’t need someone full-time, just a trustworthy person you can call when something breaks.</p></div></div>
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">3</span><div><span class="jour-k">Once it runs smoothly</span><h4>A manager or co-host</h4><p>They handle day-to-day operations: guest messages, check-ins and coordinating the team. Hire someone who lives nearby, so they can also be your emergency contact and go to the property if needed. You can also find a co-host on Airbnb itself.</p>
<div class="mini" style="margin-top:10px"><b>Do it yourself first</b>Do this role yourself for the first few weeks. Then you’ll know exactly what to delegate and what to expect.</div></div></div>
</div>
::part How to find reliable people
<p>Hiring the right people can make or break your hosting business. A great team keeps everything running smoothly. <strong>The wrong people cost you time, money and 5-star reviews.</strong></p>
<div class="vk-grid3">
<div class="vk-card"><span class="vk-n">1</span><h4>Ask other hosts</h4><p>The best hires often come from word of mouth. Join local Facebook groups for hosts or expats in your area and ask for trusted contacts.</p></div>
<div class="vk-card"><span class="vk-n">2</span><h4>Start with a test job</h4><p>Before giving someone full responsibility, try one small task, like a trial cleaning or a basic repair. Check their reliability, communication and attention to detail.</p></div>
<div class="vk-card"><span class="vk-n">3</span><h4>Be extremely clear</h4><p>People can’t read your mind. Share exactly what you expect, how and when.</p><ul><li>Use templates and checklists</li><li>Give access to your property wiki (see bonus template)</li><li>Put everything in writing and have them sign it (see the agreements below)</li></ul></div>
<div class="vk-card"><span class="vk-n">4</span><h4>Pay on time, be kind</h4><p>Reliable people stay when they’re treated well. Pay fairly and consistently, say thank you, and let them know they matter. Good relationships mean better work and longer partnerships.</p></div>
<div class="vk-card vk-wide2"><span class="vk-n">5</span><h4>Have a backup for every role</h4><p>Even the best people get sick, go on holiday or move away. Always have at least one backup cleaner, handyman and emergency contact. It keeps your business running and gives you peace of mind.</p></div>
</div>
<div class="vk-tip"><b>Good to know:</b> it’s completely normal to go through a few hires and fires before you find people who are reliable and fit your standards. Don’t get discouraged. It’s part of the process.</div>

::part Workflows for turnovers and check-ins
::h How to create efficient workflows for turnovers and check-ins
<p>Smooth turnovers and seamless check-ins are what separate amateur hosts from professional operators. <strong>If you want to scale or step away from daily management, you need systems that run without you.</strong> Turnovers and check-ins aren’t just chores: they’re your frontline guest experience, and your reviews (and revenue) will show it.</p>
<div class="vk-grid3">
<div class="vk-card"><span class="vk-n">1</span><h4>Create a turnover routine</h4><p>Hotels clean in the same proven order every time. Steal that strategy: from how to enter the apartment to the order of the rooms, a set process means nothing gets missed and new team members learn fast.</p><p class="vk-win">Templates are waiting at the end of this lesson.</p></div>
<div class="vk-card"><span class="vk-n">2</span><h4>Same products, same place</h4><p>If the towels are always in the same drawer and the coffee is always on the left shelf, your team works faster and guests don’t message you asking where things are. Keep backups in a labeled, locked space.</p></div>
<div class="vk-card"><span class="vk-n">3</span><h4>Rotate linens smartly</h4><p>Have <strong>three sets of linens per bed and three sets of towels per guest</strong>, so you’re never caught off guard on quick turnovers:</p><div class="trio"><span>1 in use</span><span>1 clean &amp; ready</span><span>1 in the wash</span></div></div>
<div class="vk-card"><span class="vk-n">4</span><h4>A lost &amp; found box</h4><p>Keep a small box in a hidden but accessible spot for anything guests leave behind. Your cleaner puts forgotten items there. Empty it once a month: throw leftovers away or give them to the cleaner.</p></div>
<div class="vk-card"><span class="vk-n">5</span><h4>Have a backup plan</h4><p>Cleaners get sick. Guests check in early. The internet goes down. Make sure you have:</p><ul><li>A backup cleaner or co-host you can call</li><li>A process for emergency maintenance</li><li>Clear SOPs anyone can follow if you’re unavailable</li></ul></div>
<div class="vk-card"><span class="vk-n">6</span><h4>Train once, delegate forever</h4><p>Build a property wiki: one place where everything about your listing lives, from how to restock to what to do if the toilet leaks. Onboarding gets fast, and questions and errors drop.</p></div>
</div>
::tip Remember: The most important tasks are mapped out for you, in order, inside your bonus “Airbnb roadmap: task checklist to launch your first listing.” <a href="#roadmap">Use it as your step-by-step guide</a> to stay on track and avoid missing anything important.
::part Managing supplies and restocking
<p>Behind the scenes, how you manage supplies and laundry can make or break the guest experience (and your sanity). <strong>The goal: no last-minute runs for toilet paper and no stress over missing sheets.</strong></p>
<div class="flow">
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">1</span><div><h4>Set up a clear inventory system</h4><p>List every essential, from toilet paper and soap to sponges, trash bags and coffee. Track how much is used per week or per guest, and set a <strong>minimum stock level</strong>. When you hit it, restock.</p><div class="mini" style="margin-top:8px"><b>Pro tip</b>Log usage and quantities in a Notion or Google Sheets tracker. You’ll find a template in the bonus resources.</div></div></div>
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">2</span><div><h4>Decide who restocks, and give them a working capital</h4><p>Decide who buys the supplies (cleaner, manager or you), how often, and how you’ll reimburse them.</p>
<div class="vk-tip"><b>Working capital:</b> a set monthly amount in cash or a digital wallet, so your team never pays out of pocket. Top it up monthly or whenever it runs low.</div>
<div class="mini" style="margin-top:8px"><b>Stay on budget</b>Keep all receipts in a shared folder or Notion tracker. Even easier: a WhatsApp group where your team posts a photo of each receipt and the remaining balance.</div></div></div>
</div>
::part SOPs, task templates and property manual
::h SOPs, task templates and property manual | to keep everything running smoothly
<p>If you want your property to run like a well-oiled machine, especially when you’re not there, <strong>SOPs (Standard Operating Procedures) are your secret weapon.</strong> They’re simply clear, written instructions for how things are done, so you never have to explain the same thing twice.</p>
<h4>The SOPs you’ll want to set up</h4>
<div class="tiles">
<div><b>[[ic:broom]]Cleaning checklist</b>Room-by-room tasks in order of priority</div>
<div><b>[[ic:refresh]]Turnover workflow</b>What happens between checkout and the next guest</div>
<div><b>[[ic:key]]Guest check-in</b>From automatic messages to key handovers or smart locks</div>
<div><b>[[ic:box]]Restocking &amp; supplies</b>What needs to be in stock at all times</div>
<div><b>[[ic:alert]]Reporting issues</b>What to do if something breaks or a guest complains</div>
<div><b>[[ic:phone]]Emergencies</b>Contacts, troubleshooting, water leaks, lost keys and so on</div>
<div class="vk-wide"><b>[[ic:calendar]]Maintenance schedule</b>Monthly or quarterly checks to avoid costly repairs</div>
</div>
<div class="gold"><span class="gold-k">Put it all in one place</span><p><strong>Your property manual.</strong> A shared Google Doc, a Notion page or a printed folder in the property, as long as your team can always find it. It’s one of the best time-saving systems you can create.</p><a class="btn" href="#manual" style="margin-top:14px">Open the property manual</a></div>
::part Labels and little details
::h Labels for clarity and consistency
<p>Clear labels keep your team organized, confuse guests less and save time on every turnover.</p>
<div class="chips"><span>✓ Everything goes back in the right place</span><span>✓ New team members learn faster</span><span>✓ More intuitive for guests</span><span>✓ Less loss or misuse of supplies</span><span>✓ Fewer daily questions</span></div>
<div class="vk-two">
<div class="vk-card"><div class="vk-tag tag-t">For guests</div><h4>What to label</h4><ul><li>Fridge shelves (with room or suite names)</li><li>Kitchen cabinets and drawers (shared vs. private)</li><li>Water dispensers (“Drinking water only”)</li><li>Trash and recycling bins</li><li>The washing machine program you recommend</li><li>Bathroom storage (“Shared” / “Private”)</li></ul></div>
<div class="vk-card"><div class="vk-tag tag-t">For your team</div><h4>What to label</h4><ul><li>Cleaning product shelves or boxes</li><li>Restock bins (“Spare towels”, “Toilet paper stock”)</li><li>The lost &amp; found box</li><li>Garden tools or pool supplies</li><li>Wash cycle instructions on the machine</li><li>Laundry baskets (“Dirty towels”, “Stained sheets”, “Ready to wash”)</li></ul></div>
</div>
<div class="vk-tip"><b>Label tips:</b> use laminated or waterproof labels in kitchens, bathrooms and outdoors. Add icons or color codes if your team speaks different languages (free icons at <a href="https://www.flaticon.com" target="_blank" rel="noopener">flaticon.com</a>). Keep font, size and wording consistent, and a simple laminating machine or label maker for renewals. <strong>Keep it simple:</strong> “Suite 1 shelf”, “Refills only”, “Not for guests”. One language plus icons, never a wall of text.</div>
::h Little details that save big headaches
<p>Once your operations are running, it’s the tiny things no one thinks to document that cause the biggest slowdowns. <strong>These extra details save you hours of messages and fire-fighting:</strong></p>
<div class="tiles">
<div><b>[[ic:washer]]Label the washing machine</b>We put a ‘WASH’ label on the right program, so guests don’t pick a 3-hour cycle. It saves energy, and many people don’t know how to use one.</div>
<div><b>[[ic:coffee]]Label how things work</b>The coffee machine, the heater… especially in shared apartments.</div>
<div><b>[[ic:camera]]Photograph everything</b>Electricity box, boiler, heating controls, Wi-Fi router. Store the photos in your manual for when you need to explain something.</div>
<div><b>[[ic:bed]]One extra set of protectors</b>Spare pillow protectors and mattress covers per property, so a bed is never left unprotected.</div>
<div><b>[[ic:pan]]Stainless steel pans</b>They last longer and look clean for longer.</div>
<div><b>[[ic:spoon]]Stock up on small spoons</b>They always go missing. Always.</div>
<div class="vk-wide"><b>[[ic:recycle]]Visual trash instructions</b>Labeled photos or icons that show guests exactly where and how to separate their waste.</div>
</div>
<div class="gold"><span class="gold-k">Pro tip</span><p><strong>“Something isn’t working”? Ask for a quick video first.</strong> Or jump on a video call before sending maintenance. Often it isn’t broken, it’s just being used the wrong way. This one step saves time, unnecessary callouts and helps you diagnose faster.</p></div>
::part The shared spaces special
::h The shared spaces special | Essential things you need to know
<p>Shared apartments, where guests have private rooms but share the kitchen, bathroom or living room, can be very profitable. <strong>But they come with their own rules, systems and friction points.</strong> Here’s how to manage them smoothly:</p>
<div class="vk-steps vk-nonum">
<div class="vk-step"><h4>Clear boundaries from day one</h4><p>Add clear house rules to your listing.</p></div>
<div class="vk-step"><h4>Private vs. shared, in writing</h4><p>Be specific in your listing and messages. Add photos, floor plans and the rules for shared areas (quiet hours, no personal items in common spaces).</p></div>
<div class="vk-step"><h4>A lock on every bedroom door</h4><p>Lockable from inside and outside. Non-negotiable for safety, privacy and trust.</p></div>
<div class="vk-step"><h4>Dedicated, labeled spaces</h4><p>Label shelves, drawers and even fridge sections in shared kitchens and bathrooms.</p></div>
<div class="vk-step"><h4>Clean more often</h4><p>Even daily in high-turnover properties. Cleanliness is one of the top review killers in shared setups.</p></div>
<div class="vk-step"><h4>Keep extras</h4><p>Towels, toilet paper, small utensils and mugs, so there’s enough for everyone.</p></div>
<div class="vk-step vk-wide"><h4>Resolve conflicts fast</h4><p>If one guest complains about another, respond fast, stay neutral and offer a solution, like changing rooms or reinforcing the rules. The quick replies in <a href="#ch9">Lesson 9</a> have templates for this.</p></div>
</div>
<div class="vk-tip"><b>Pro tip:</b> put a friendly house rules sign (or a mini welcome booklet) in the living room or kitchen. It’s a subtle way to guide behavior and avoid awkward situations.</div>
::h The 3-step decluttering process for shared spaces
<p>Over time, shared kitchens and bathrooms fill up with things previous guests left behind. That means mess, confusion and even hygiene issues. <strong>Reset and declutter at least every 3 months</strong>, without removing items that are still in use. First, pick your method:</p>
<div class="vk-two">
<div class="vk-card"><div class="vk-tag tag-t">Method A</div><h4>The “lost &amp; found” system</h4><p>Choose one spot for leftover items, like a basket in the bathroom or a freezer drawer. Anything placed there is removed after a set deadline. Great for shampoo bottles, freezer food and forgotten belongings.</p></div>
<div class="vk-card"><div class="vk-tag tag-t">Method B</div><h4>The label system</h4><p>Leave tape and a marker near shared storage (fridge door, pantry). Guests label their items with their name. Anything unlabeled gets cleared out.</p></div>
</div>
<div class="chips"><span class="chips-k">Key areas:</span><span>Shower bottles</span><span>Freezer &amp; fridge door</span><span>Unlabeled drawers &amp; shelves</span><span>Random items in common areas</span></div>
<div class="flow">
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">1</span><div><h4>Start with a deep clean</h4><p>Clean the kitchen, bathroom and hallway. It’s the best moment to spot forgotten items, expired food and things taking up space. Then move them to your removal spot, or start labeling.</p></div></div>
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">2</span><div><h4>Communicate clearly and kindly</h4><p>Even if you spoke to someone in person, send a message with the method and a short deadline. Send a reminder the day before you remove anything.</p></div></div>
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">3</span><div><h4>Set a clear removal date</h4><p>Give people 48–72 hours to claim their things, then remove what’s left. Optional: let remaining items be “adopted” before they’re thrown away.</p></div></div>
</div>
<p>You can send these as a WhatsApp, Airbnb or group message after the deep clean:</p>

::part Staying legally compliant while scaling
<p>One listing is simple. Several cities or countries? <strong>Compliance gets more complex, and more important.</strong> You already know the basics from <a href="#ch2">Lesson 2</a>. Here’s your checklist for every new location:</p>
<div class="flow">
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">1</span><div><h4>Research the local rental laws</h4><div class="chips" style="margin:6px 0 8px"><span>Are short stays legal here?</span><span>Night limits per year?</span><span>Zoning restrictions?</span><span>Do you need to live on-site?</span></div><div class="mini"><b>Ask locals</b>Local host Facebook groups and the Airbnb Community Center are great for hearing about recent changes.</div></div></div>
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">2</span><div><h4>Get the right licenses and registrations</h4><p>Many cities require a business license, a short-term rental permit, a registration number on your listing and emergency contact info on-site. <div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">3</span><div><h4>Understand your taxes</h4>
<div class="vk-two vk-tight"><div class="mini"><b>You may owe</b>Tourist or occupancy tax, income tax (local and foreign if you manage from abroad), and VAT or sales tax in some countries.</div><div class="mini"><b>You may need to</b>Register as a business, file monthly or quarterly returns, and keep every receipt and expense report.</div></div>
<p style="margin-top:10px">Work with an accountant who knows short-term rentals in each country you operate in.</p></div></div>
<div class="flow-step"><span class="vk-n">4</span><div><h4>Follow guest safety rules</h4><div class="chips" style="margin:6px 0 8px"><span>Smoke &amp; CO detectors</span><span>Fire extinguisher</span><span>First-aid kit</span><span>Marked exits</span><span>Insurance</span></div><p>Even where it’s not mandatory, it protects you and your guests, and may be needed for Airbnb’s host protection.</p></div></div>

Hi everyone,
We did a deep clean and we’ll be removing items left behind by previous tenants to declutter, keep things clean, and free up space for you.

To make sure we don’t accidentally remove anything that belongs to you, we kindly ask you to do the following by [specific date]

🔹 Shower:
Please place your items either in the soap basket on the wall or on your dedicated shelf.
🔹 Freezer:
Move your items out of the top drawer
🔹 Fridge door:
Place your items on your shelf or label them using tape (there’s a roll on top of the microwave).
🔹 Hallway box:
There’s a box in the hallway with food & items we found outside of personal shelves.
If anything is yours, feel free to take it back!

Once everyone has collected their things, any remaining items can be “adopted” [the day before the deadline] if you’d like to keep something.

⚠ On [specific date] all unclaimed items will be removed, including:
- Items on the shower floor
- Items in the fridge door without tape
- Food in the top freezer drawer
- Items in the hallway box

Thanks a lot for your help and cooperation!
::end

Hi everyone,

Just a quick final reminder that tomorrow is the deadline to claim your items as part of the decluttering process.

We’ll be removing the following:
- Items left on the shower floor
- Unlabeled items in the fridge door

::lesson bonus-mistakes | ★ | Common mistakes | Bonus · learn from ours | Common | mistakes | The mistakes we see hosts make most, and how to avoid them.
<p>Whether you’re just getting started or already managing several listings, these are the pitfalls that cost hosts the most time, money and peace of mind. <strong>We made most of them ourselves, so you don’t have to.</strong></p>
<h3>Guests &amp; bookings</h3>
<div class="mk-grid">
<div class="mk"><span class="mk-x">✕</span><h4>[[ic:key]]Unclear late-night check-ins</h4><p>Guests arriving after midnight run into more problems. Make your self-check-in foolproof, or expect urgent calls at 2am.</p><a href="#ch12">Self-check-in, Lesson 12 →</a></div>
<div class="mk"><span class="mk-x">✕</span><h4>[[ic:calendar]]Accepting “change” requests to dodge fees</h4><p>Some guests ask to shorten their stay only to avoid the cancellation fee. Decline and stick to your policy. It protects your income.</p></div>
<div class="mk"><span class="mk-x">✕</span><h4>[[ic:alert]]Bookings outside the platform</h4><p>Saving on fees is tempting, but it removes all protection for you and the guest. Keep messages and payments on the platform.</p></div>
<div class="mk"><span class="mk-x">✕</span><h4>[[ic:sparkle]]Taking it personally</h4><p>Most guests are amazing, but once in a while one is simply unfair. Stay calm, stay professional and stick to the facts.</p><a href="#ch9">Handling complaints, Lesson 9 →</a></div>
</div>
<h3>Your team &amp; money</h3>
<div class="mk-grid">
<div class="mk"><span class="mk-x">✕</span><h4>[[ic:camera]]No photos from your cleaner</h4><p>If something is damaged or missing (even towels), photos are your evidence for claims and disputes. Make it part of every turnover.</p></div>
<div class="mk"><span class="mk-x">✕</span><h4>[[ic:refresh]]Expecting the perfect hire first time</h4><p>Most people won’t be a perfect fit on the first try. Don’t be afraid to change until your team is reliable.</p><a href="#ch10">Build your team, Lesson 10 →</a></div>
<div class="mk"><span class="mk-x">✕</span><h4>[[ic:phone]]One person doing everything</h4><p>Split communication, finances and maintenance, so nothing falls apart when someone is unavailable.</p></div>
<div class="mk"><span class="mk-x">✕</span><h4>[[ic:box]]Sharing all your numbers</h4><p>Not everyone on your team needs to see your earnings. Too much information can create tension or unrealistic expectations.</p></div>
</div>
<h3>In the property</h3>
<div class="mk-grid">
<div class="mk"><span class="mk-x">✕</span><h4>[[ic:key]]No spare key or backup access</h4><p>Keys get lost or stolen. Without a plan B you risk lockouts, unhappy guests and expensive emergency locksmiths.</p></div>
<div class="mk"><span class="mk-x">✕</span><h4>[[ic:washer]]Unlabeled washing machine</h4><p>Put a clear sticker or arrow on the right program. Otherwise guests pick the 3-hour cycle, which costs you more and blocks the machine.</p></div>
<div class="mk"><span class="mk-x">✕</span><h4>[[ic:bulb]]Long messages and labels</h4><p>Travelers are tired and skip long texts. Keep scheduled messages and labels short and to the point.</p></div>
<div class="mk"><span class="mk-x">✕</span><h4>[[ic:sparkle]]Fragile wine glasses</h4><p>They always break. Keep extras in stock, or switch to more durable ones.</p></div>
<div class="mk"><span class="mk-x">✕</span><h4>[[ic:bed]]Bulky storage</h4><p>Store duvets and other bulky items in vacuum bags. They take less space and stay clean.</p></div>
</div>
<div class="gold"><span class="gold-k">The takeaway</span><p><strong>Avoid these, and your hosting gets smoother, safer and more professional.</strong> Most of them take five minutes to fix today.</p></div>
::endlesson
