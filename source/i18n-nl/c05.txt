@@639
2. Demand for the property itself
@@640
This is the part most new hosts miss. {1}Many guests don’t travel for the city at all. They travel for the stay.{2} They are not looking for a bed close to the supermarket or the metro. They are looking for an experience, and they are happy to drive an extra hour to get it.
@@641
When the property is the reason for the trip, a location that looks “bad” on paper stops being a problem. It can even become the selling point: quiet, private, surrounded by nature, far from everything.
@@642
A cabin or treehouse in the woods, an hour from the nearest city, that couples book for a weekend with no phone signal.
@@643
A villa in the middle of rice fields, 20 minutes from the beach and the restaurants, that guests choose for the view, the private pool and the silence.
@@644
A design house in a small village with nothing around it, booked for birthdays and anniversaries because of how it looks in the photos.
@@645
A farm stay or countryside house with a hot tub, a fireplace and animals, where families come to switch off for a few days.
@@646
In all of these, nobody searched for the village. They found a place they wanted to stay in, and then planned the trip around it. This also means less competition: you are not one of 300 similar apartments in the same neighborhood, you are the only place like yours.
@@647
So which one do you have?
@@648
{1}Don’t just chase the obvious tourist hotspots. {2}For every property you look at, ask:
@@649
Who is coming here, and why?
@@650
Are they coming for the area, or would they come for this property?
@@651
When is demand highest?
@@652
What kind of stays are most common: short trips, weekend getaways, monthly relocations?
@@653
If the location is average, what could make the stay special: a view, a pool, a hot tub, the design, total privacy?
@@654
Use AirROI and Airbnb’s own search filters to explore demand in specific areas. Look at occupancy rates, average nightly rates, and competition. Also search Airbnb for unusual stays in your region and check how booked their calendars are.
@@655
The goal isn’t just to be in a “good area.” The goal is to have the right property for the right guest: either in a place people already want to be, or special enough that people come for it.

@@680

Red flags to avoid when choosing a property
@@717
Not all properties are good for renting out to guests, whether you host short-term or mid-term stays, even if they look beautiful, cheap, or perfectly located. Some places come with hidden issues that can cost you time, money, and a ton of stress. The key is learning to spot these red flags before you commit.
@@718
Here’s what to watch out for:
@@719
Strict building rules or HOAs
@@720
If you’re looking at an apartment in a building with a homeowner’s association (HOA), make sure to ask about their rules for short-term rentals and subletting. Some buildings ban or heavily restrict short stays, while mid-term stays of a month or more are often fine. With short stays, a concierge or nosy neighbors will notice guest traffic quickly.
